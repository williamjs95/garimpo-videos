# Process and metrics: how studios and short-form creators improve from one episode to the next

Research date: 2026-11-22. Source status tags used on every finding:

- **[search summary]** = I only saw a search-engine summary/snippet of the page; wording and details are unverified.
- **[platform]** = the platform's own documentation or statement. **[vendor]** = a company that sells social/video tools (commercial interest, methods usually undisclosed). **[independent]** = journalism, academic, or practitioner account with no product to sell on the topic.

## 1. Studio review practices (Pixar dailies, Braintrust, plussing, Disney sweatbox, post-mortems, TV triage, quality bar, banking reusable improvements)

### Takeaway
The documented studio pattern is: show unfinished work early and often, get candid notes from people with no authority over the maker, record notes in writing, and separate "errors" from "creative changes" so polish has a stopping rule. For TV schedules the explicit stopping rule I found is quantitative (approve when only 3-5% of shots are still in a third retake cycle) and the explicit warning is that beyond that point "pursuing perfection may cost more than the value it delivers."

### Cited Findings

**Pixar Braintrust**
- Catmull: "the Braintrust should have no authority. Our group could not tell the director what to do." A copy of the Braintrust for technical teams failed, and Catmull traced the failure to that group having authority. [opened] [independent, interview-based] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/spark-creativity-with-these-tips-from-pixars-president/)
- Catmull on why it worked: "There was something about having colleagues you trusted so much giving notes to each other that worked really well"; "They would have these intense discussions, but it never got personal." [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/spark-creativity-with-these-tips-from-pixars-president/)
- Catmull on iteration: "First conclusions are almost always wrong, and so are the second and the third"; "Don't judge the ideas until you have to"; "more ideas yield better ideas." [opened] — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/spark-creativity-with-these-tips-from-pixars-president/)
- Catmull says Pixar films "suck" early on; the Braintrust meets every few months, members "don't hold back"; example of a note that changed WALL-E's ending (Brad Bird to Andrew Stanton: "You've denied your audience the moment they've been waiting for"). Note the form of that note: it names what the audience is missing, it does not prescribe the fix. [opened] (2014 Kotaku article summarizing a Fast Company excerpt of Creativity, Inc.; second-hand) (pre-2023) — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/what-pixar-does-to-keep-their-movies-from-sucking-1542456993)
- "The director does not have to follow any of the specific suggestions given." [search summary] — [Stanford eCorner transcript, "Inside the Braintrust"](https://stvp.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/09/inside-the-braintrust-transcript.pdf)

**Pixar dailies**
- Dailies: artists on a project meet at the start of the day to present work for feedback. Benefits listed in a summary of Creativity, Inc.: prevents over-commitment to one's own work, enables collaborative problem-solving, catches problems "before hundreds of hours go into a project", gives everyone a voice, promotes candor that reveals flaws the maker was blind to. Changes discussed range "from the size of a stick in the background to an entire character model." No direct Catmull quote on the page. [opened] (book summary site, secondary) — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/pixar-dailies/)

**Pixar post-mortems**
- Catmull lists "five reasons" to do postmortems; one is to "consolidate what's been learned"; he says "hindsight is not 20-20" and that measuring the outcome without evaluating the process is deceiving; postmortems should identify not only what went wrong but what went right for reasons the team did not understand. [search summary] (secondary notes on Creativity, Inc.; not verified against the book) — [SKMurphy](https://skmurphy.com/?p=141), [Haacked reading notes](https://gist.github.com/haacked/97483eca87c3e1daac9f), [Motion Picture Association summary](https://www.motionpictures.org/2014/05/lessons-gleaned-from-pixar-co-founder-ed-catmulls-creativity-inc/)

**Plussing**
- Definition found in secondary sources: a criticism must come with a "plus", i.e. a suggestion that builds on or strengthens the idea. Sources DISAGREE on origin: one credits Walt Disney with coining the term "more than 60 years ago"; another says Pixar took Disney's plussing and added two improv concepts; a third says Pixar borrowed it from improv comedy. [search summary] — [ERE](https://www.ere.net/articles/plus-your-way-to-successful-feedback), [Intense Minimalism](https://intenseminimalism.com/2015/pixars-plussing-technique-of-giving-feedback/), [LEADx](https://leadx.org/articles/pixar/)

**Disney sweatbox**
- The room where Walt Disney reviewed pencil tests was nicknamed "The Sweatbox" by the animators under scrutiny; the word became the name of the review process. [search summary] — [Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/john-canemaker-unlocks-the-secrets-of-fantasias-sweatbox-notes-109312.html), [FLIP blog](https://flipanimation.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-orthodox-sweatbox-paradox.html)
- Mechanics, from surviving Fantasia notes analyzed by John Canemaker: Disney plus supervisors and directors reviewed each scene with the animators present; scenes were "frankly dissected and discussed"; a stenographer recorded the comments, which were typed and distributed to all participants. Notes were addressed by name and by role (lead animator, effects, assistant, checker, layout). [opened] (historical, pre-2023) — [Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/john-canemaker-unlocks-the-secrets-of-fantasias-sweatbox-notes-109312.html)
- One scene (Fantasia scene 23) had dated notes on 28 Feb, 9 Apr, 13 Apr and a follow-up five days later, and was marked "O.K. for final check" on 19 Jul 1940, i.e. about five months of recorded review rounds for one scene. Note types: timing/staging ("slow the fish's entrance"), technical instruction ("We want this scintillation animation put on two's"), requests for two camera tests before deciding, assistant-level cleanup, final approval with one last small request. Canemaker characterizes them as "marching orders from generals to troops in the trenches" with "extensive checks and balances." [opened] — [Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/john-canemaker-unlocks-the-secrets-of-fantasias-sweatbox-notes-109312.html)

**TV animation under tight schedules: what gets fixed and when to stop**
- Retakes fall into four types with different root causes: careless mistakes; unmet references/style guide; artistic or creative changes; notes "not addressed correctly." Categorize before acting. [opened] [vendor: CG-Wire sells production-tracking software] — [CG-Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show)
- Creative changes should be labeled and tracked separately from technical ones: "Treat them as scope changes, not routine retakes." [opened] [vendor] — [CG-Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show)
- Stopping rule: treat an episode as approved once only "3-5% of shots remain in a third retake cycle"; "At that point, pursuing perfection may cost more than the value it delivers." [opened] [vendor] — [CG-Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show)
- Metric to track: percentage of shots in retake, split by first/second/third round; investigate if retake rates stay high after the first few episodes. Retake work counts against the same capacity as new shots. [opened] [vendor] — [CG-Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show)
- Senior reviewers should spend their time on early rounds "where their feedback has the greatest impact"; small fixes are faster done in-house than sent back with another round of notes, then documented as a training example. [opened] [vendor] — [CG-Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show)
- Bob's Burgers: the animatic is built with the episodic director and shipped to South Korea; "Roughly twelve weeks later, we get back the first of two color batches." At the rough-color screening, editors pre-fix small things (eye acting, sound effects, pacing) because "we want the showrunners to focus on the narrative." Notes then go to the writers' room for a final rewrite; "Often there's anywhere between 60 and 280 lines of dialogue that we'll cut in," shifting timing by 15-20 seconds either way. [opened] [independent: Motion Picture Editors Guild magazine] — [CineMontage](https://cinemontage.org/how-the-burger-is-made-the-workflow-secrets-of-bobs-burgers/)
- Same source, cheap-fix-first logic: "If a shot has ADR that matches what the shipped audio was, it may be possible to avert a retake by fixing lip sync"; every shot is analyzed "to see if it requires an animation conform"; existing animation cycles are reused where they fit; when a shot is too short "we have to make our own handles" by choosing frames to hold. [opened] — [CineMontage](https://cinemontage.org/how-the-burger-is-made-the-workflow-secrets-of-bobs-burgers/)
- Bento Box (Krapopolis): the retakes team is described as the final hands in post, carrying the director's notes into the finished show. A Keyframe interviewee's week starts by watching the animatic and returned retake animation, then pulling scenes that carry notes. [search summary] — [Toon Boom](https://www.toonboom.com/?p=17996), [Keyframe Magazine](https://keyframemagazine.org/?p=455)
- Anime: teams start with two months or more per episode but "schedule creep" sets in after a few episodes. [search summary] (2018, pre-2023) — [Anime News Network Answerman](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/answerman/2018-03-16/.129066)

**Banking reusable improvements**
- Solo indie animator DeepBlueInk (Deep Space Discounts): rigs evolved from his first rig; master controllers "are built off of the same twelve or so frames"; he adopted Master Controller specifically to save time; rigs are planned with turns in mind. He budgets time as fixed output: about 90-120 seconds of animation per week, kept up for about five years. [opened] [vendor-hosted interview: Toon Boom] — [Toon Boom](https://www.toonboom.com/?p=18242)

### Inferences
- For a one-person show the Braintrust principle translates to: reviewers (a friend, a test viewer, an AI critic) describe what is unclear/missing for the audience; the maker alone decides the fix. The WALL-E note is the model: a statement about the audience's experience, not a prescription.
- The sweatbox contribution worth copying is the written, addressed, dated note: a per-episode log of notes (what, where in the video, which pipeline stage owns it) is what lets improvements be tracked across episodes instead of rediscovered.
- CG-Wire's split (execution error vs. creative change) maps onto a solo pipeline as "defect in this episode" (fix now if cheap) vs. "change to the recipe" (a scope change: decide deliberately, apply from the next episode, and bank it in the template/guide/prompt).
- Bob's Burgers' pattern (fix eye acting, SFX and pacing before the showrunner screening so judgment goes to narrative) suggests ordering the solo review: clear mechanical defects first, then judge the joke.
- Both TV sources put the highest-leverage review before expensive work (animatic stage). In this project the equivalent is the script and the audio-timed cut before rendering.
- A "quality bar per episode" is implied by the 3-5% rule and by DeepBlueInk's fixed weekly output budget, rather than stated as a named concept (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- I could not open Creativity, Inc., the Fast Company excerpt or the HBR 2008 article. The often-repeated details (Braintrust notes should be specific and not prescriptive; postmortem technique of listing "top five things we'd do again / top five we wouldn't"; the full list of five reasons for postmortems; "from suck to not-suck"; Notes Day) are NOT verified here. A search explicitly found no source for the "top five" phrasing. Check the book before quoting.
- No primary source confirmed for "plussing" (origin disputed across secondary sources; not confirmed as a term used in Catmull's book).
- No detailed first-hand account found of Walt Disney's own 1930s sweatbox sessions beyond the Fantasia notes.
- No source found that uses "quality bar per episode" or A/B/C shot-priority tiers as a documented TV-animation practice; the search for it returned nothing specific.
- No verified numbers on number of retakes per episode for any named show.

## 2. Iteration under constraint (Flash-era series, YouTube animators, TikTok animated micro-series)

### Takeaway
Creator accounts consistently describe improvement as a by-product of a fixed cadence plus reuse: the same process repeated weekly or daily, steps dropped as they become unnecessary, and audio treated as the spine. I found little hard evidence on "what gave the most visible gain per hour"; what exists is self-report.

### Cited Findings
- Homestar Runner (Brothers Chaps): "We do one cartoon a week. We think of it, write it, animate it, and it's done pretty quickly"; the process for their later series is "the exact same process." Early on they were "just trying to learn Flash." Output: "four or five hundred cartoons that are all largely tied together." [opened] [independent] (2017 oral history, pre-2023) — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/an-oral-history-of-homestar-runner-the-internets-favor-1791519879)
- Cost of the cadence: Mike Chapman recalls "20 hours straight of sitting in front of a computer bleary-eyed on Sunday night"; "We probably stuck to that weekly schedule a little more strictly than we needed to, so we needed a break"; "It was definitely a slog sometimes." [opened] — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/an-oral-history-of-homestar-runner-the-internets-favor-1791519879)
- They stopped tracking site stats so the numbers "didn't affect how we made stuff"; audience signal came from emails (angry mail about late cartoons: "it hit us that folks were counting on new stuff from us"); Matt Chapman noticed the moments between competitions were funnier than the competitions, which reshaped the show. [opened] — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/an-oral-history-of-homestar-runner-the-internets-favor-1791519879)
- Nutshell Animations (described by Tubefilter in 2022 as TikTok's most popular animator): batches "a week's worth of daily content roughly within three or four days"; each piece takes "between three to six hours"; after about two and a half to three years he skips earlier extra passes: "I can kind of skip all that now because the flow of it is easier for me"; treats daily posting as practice: "I'm kind of sharpening my scales every single day." Growth came after switching from weekly/semiweekly longer story pieces to daily short animations on a new account with a daily commitment. Uses comments to compare platforms: "You can see amongst the comments what's working for one side, what's not working for the other." [opened] [independent] (2022, pre-2023) — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2022/08/11/youtube-millionaires-nutshell-animations/)
- DeepBlueInk: for YouTube shorts he takes the audio, puts it straight into Toon Boom Harmony and draws over a transparent storyboard. "For the first four years of working full-time I didn't storyboard my animations." Later became "less precious" about boards: "they are the roughest, tiniest thumbs, but they do the job perfectly." Rated about twenty character doodles with four colored pens (great/good/okay/bad) and kept six. [opened] [vendor-hosted interview] — [Toon Boom](https://www.toonboom.com/?p=18242)
- TikTok animators (Cartoon Brew, Dec 2020): Gottfried Roodt (Noodle and Bun): "we kept posting every day, or every second day"; "we've learned in two months what would have taken years in any other medium"; the platform is good for experiments because there are "no consequences other than getting fewer likes than usual." TootyMcNooty: early animatics "were red line drawings, simpler than my current videos"; it is "fun to test out a bunch of different ideas in short videos." [opened] [independent] (2020, pre-2023) — [Cartoon Brew](https://cartoonbrew.com/apps/meet-the-animators-who-are-reaching-millions-of-viewers-on-tiktok-199436.html)
- Audio-first: a production guide argues "animation should be built around the soundtrack"; an explainer producer who skipped the animatic reported that re-timing every keyframe after re-recording audio cost more than the animatic would have. [search summary] — [Nalena](https://nalena.com/process-of-animation/), [Wistia](https://wistia.com/learn/production/animated-video-interview)
- AI-assisted TikTok series: a profile of a software engineer's animated series says character consistency across sketches is the hard problem and recommends building and testing characters as still images first. [search summary] [vendor: Neolemon sells an AI character tool] — [Neolemon](https://www.neolemon.com/creator-stories/software-engineer-builds-animated-series-felix-and-barry)
- Leaked MrBeast production onboarding document (Sept 2024, authenticity unconfirmed; long-form, not animation): the team works to three metrics, CTR, AVD (average view duration) and AVP (average view percentage), and treats the first minute as make-or-break. [search summary] — [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/), [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2024/09/17/mrbeast-internal-production-guide-leaked-key-points/)

### Inferences
- The recurring self-reported order of improvement is: (1) commit to a cadence, (2) simplify and reuse (rigs, cycles, same world and characters), (3) drop steps that stopped paying, (4) raise drawing/animation polish last. TootyMcNooty and Nutshell both started simpler than their current work and improved while publishing.
- High cadence is itself the feedback mechanism (Roodt: two months of posting taught what would have taken years). At three episodes a week this project gets about 150 feedback cycles a year, which favors small per-episode changes over big redesigns.
- Homestar Runner is a counter-example to metric-driven work (they deliberately stopped looking at stats) and a warning on cadence rigidity leading to burnout. Their useful signals were qualitative: which moments were funnier than the planned ones.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies "gain per hour" of specific improvements (audio vs. lip-sync vs. backgrounds). Everything here is anecdote.
- Nothing found on Simon's Cat, Salad Fingers or other Flash-era shows regarding reusable symbol libraries; nothing found from Cartoon Brew/AWN/GDC/CTN talks on short-form iteration specifically.
- No documented post-mortems found from TikTok animated micro-series produced with AI (2025-2026); the one profile found is vendor content.
- Most creator interviews found are 2017-2022 and predate current platform analytics.

## 3. Rubrics and checklists

### Takeaway
Published rubrics for animation are thin and mostly holistic: the 11 Second Club uses a single 1-11 score with one rule (judge the acting, ignore rendering), and festival criteria are three broad questions. The most usable itemized criteria come from critique guidance (readability of poses, silhouette, gesture matching voice, lip-sync, eye focus). I found no published pre-publish quality checklist from a studio or platform.

### Cited Findings
- 11 Second Club scale: 1-2 Poor, 3-4 Decent, 5-6 Good, 7-8 Great!, 9-10 Excellent!, 11 "I'll never wash my eyes again." Voters pick what they consider "the best for that month"; no further criteria are defined. Entries are shown least-rated first "to ensure fairness," and voters cannot choose which entry to rate. [opened] [independent community] — [11 Second Club FAQ](https://11secondclub.com/home/FAQ)
- What counts: "Unrendered previews or playblasts are perfectly acceptable"; "As long as we can see your acting choices clearly, that's all that matters"; "People will focus on your character animation skills; everything else may distract from your animation." [opened] — [11 Second Club FAQ](https://11secondclub.com/home/FAQ)
- 11 Second Club critique guidance ("The Compliment Sandwich"): open with a genuine strength ("Find that good thing and mention it first"), put the critique in the middle, close positive; critique against the animator's intent ("Use your best judgement to see what they were going for, and then base your critique on that"); frame problems as readability for a viewer who lacks the maker's context. Example criteria used in the text: poses held long enough to read ("You aren't holding your poses long enough for us to read what they are"); silhouette (overlapping hands reading as an unintended action); gesture scale vs. vocal tone ("big, broad gestures that don't seem to match the soft tone of the voice track"); lip-sync precision; eye focus ("the eyes drift all over the place instead of resting on one focal point at a time"); whether an added gag fits the shot's tone. [opened] — [11 Second Club Helpful Hints](https://www.11secondclub.com/helpful_hints/the_compliment_sandwich)
- Japan Media Arts Festival, Animation Division (19th edition) selection criteria: "Whether or not the work was convincing as an independent piece of short animation; whether or not there were expressions unique to animation; and whether or not it had interesting content." [search summary] (one year's jury critique, pre-2023) — [Japan Media Arts Festival](https://j-mediaarts.jp/en/award/critiques/unique-evaluation-criteria-for-animation/)
- University of Manchester schools animation competition: the team "awards points based on the criteria below" (point breakdown not visible in the snippet). [search summary] (2015, pre-2023) — [Manchester Animation15](https://curation.cs.manchester.ac.uk/Animation/Animation15/animation15.cs.manchester.ac.uk/competition/judging/index.html)
- Oscars animated short rules are eligibility rules (running time 40 minutes or less), not judging criteria. [search summary] — [Academy rules PDF](https://www.oscars.org/sites/oscars/files/2024-04/97_animated_short_rules.pdf)
- A psychologist's critique of the compliment sandwich exists ("No one wants to eat your compliment sandwich"), so the format is contested for giving feedback to others. [search summary, title only] — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/creative-leadership/201808/no-one-wants-to-eat-your-compliment-sandwich)

### Inferences
- The 11 Second Club rule ("everything else may distract from your animation") is a prioritization statement: judge the performance and the read first, surface polish last. For a comedy short the analogue is: does the joke read, is the acting clear, before backgrounds or effects.
- A workable per-episode rubric can combine one holistic score (as the Club does) with a short list of readability checks taken from the critique guidance: pose hold length, silhouette, gesture vs. voice match, lip-sync, eye focus, tone fit.
- The festival question "is it convincing as an independent piece?" matters for a series fed by algorithmic feeds: each episode must work for a viewer who has seen no other episode.
- The "least-rated first, cannot choose" voting design is a reminder to review episodes blind to their view counts where possible, to avoid judging quality by reach.

### Gaps
- No film-school grading rubric with weighted criteria was found (searches returned eligibility rules and one-off jury statements).
- No published studio or platform "pre-publish checklist" for short-form animation found. Vendor blog checklists exist in abundance but I did not find one with evidence behind it.
- No rubric specific to short comedy sketches found in this research pass (the project's own guide and the earlier comedy report cover that ground).
- The 12 principles of animation are widely used as a critique frame, but I did not retrieve a source for that in this pass.

## 4. Metrics as a diagnosis tool on TikTok, Reels and Shorts (state as of 2026)

### Takeaway
All three platforms now expose the same three diagnostic layers: an early-exit rate (YouTube "stayed to watch / viewed vs swiped away", Instagram "skip rate" for the first 3 seconds, TikTok's per-second retention graph), a retention curve, and per-view engagement rates. View counts became less comparable in 2025-2026 because YouTube now counts a view at the first frame; platform-published benchmarks do not exist, so every "good" threshold in circulation is vendor or creator data.

### Cited Findings

**YouTube Shorts [platform]**
- "Stayed to watch" / how viewers engaged: "The percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short." [opened] — [YouTube Help: content performance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281), [YouTube Help: reach](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314355?hl=en)
- "Viewed (vs. swiped away): The percentage of times viewers viewed your Shorts versus swiped away." [search summary of the YouTube Help page; when I opened the two help pages above the label shown was "stayed to watch", so the label may have been renamed or differ by page] — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281)
- Engaged views: "How many times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops." Average view duration: "Average minutes watched among those who stayed to watch," "calculated from engaged views and their corresponding watch time." Average percentage viewed: "Average percentage of a video watched among those who stayed to watch." Key moments for audience retention: "This report shows how well different moments of your video held viewers' attention." [opened] — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281)
- Consequence of those definitions: on Shorts, average view duration and average percentage viewed exclude people who swiped away at the start, so the early-exit rate and the retention averages are two separate measurements. [opened; this is a direct reading of the definitions] — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281)
- View counting: since 31 March 2025 a Shorts view is counted whenever a Short starts or replays, with no minimum watch time; the old count became "engaged views." [search summary] — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted), [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/03/26/youtube-shorts-views-counting-stats/)
- From 24 August 2026 "views across YouTube are counted from the first frame going forward" for all formats; the former definition is the engaged view. YouTube says there is no change to recommendations, analytics "remain anchored on engaged views," and CTR, average view duration and retention are calculated as before. [opened] — [YouTube Blog, 19 Aug 2026](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/engaged-views-youtube-explained/)
- YouTube publishes no official benchmarks for viewed vs swiped away, retention or other Shorts metrics. [search summary; consistent with the help pages I opened, which contain none] — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281)
- Traffic sources for Shorts are reported separately ("Shorts: Traffic from the Shorts vertical viewing experience"; "Sound pages"). [opened] — [YouTube Help: reach](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314355?hl=en)

**Instagram Reels [platform, via trade press]**
- August 2025: Instagram added a per-Reel Retention chart. Instagram: "With Retention, you'll always see a downward slope, but the flatter the line, the more engaged your audience is." [opened] — [Social Media Today, 24 Aug 2025](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-adds-retention-insights-reels/758464/)
- Skip rate replaced view rate: "skip rate is the percentage of views from people who decided to skip your reel during those first 3 seconds." View rate had measured the share who kept watching past three seconds. [opened] — [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-adds-retention-insights-reels/758464/)
- The chart starts at 100% for the first second, since that marks the start of a Reel view; skip rate = views that skipped in the first 3 seconds divided by initial views. [search summary] [vendor] — [Metricool](https://metricool.com/instagram-reel-analytics/)
- Adam Mosseri, January 2025: "The top three signals that matter most for ranking are watch time, likes and sends"; "pay close attention to average watch time, likes per reach, and sends per reach"; "Likes are slightly more important for connected content, and sends are slightly more important for unconnected content." [opened] — [Social Media Today, 22 Jan 2025](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-2025/738034/)
- A widely repeated claim that sends weigh "3 to 5 times" likes is attributed to a vendor (CreatorFlow), not to Instagram. [search summary] [vendor] — [dev.to post citing it](https://dev.to/stklen/instagram-reels-algorithm-2026-the-3-official-ranking-factors-mme)

**TikTok [platform, partially verified]**
- Creator analytics list total views, likes, comments, shares, average watch time and "watched the full video (percentage of viewers who watched the entire video)." [search summary; the Creator Academy page itself returned no content when opened] — [TikTok Creator Academy](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/tool-analytics-intro)
- TikTok ads help center definitions (ads product, but the only TikTok definitions I could open): video views = "Number of times your video started to play" (replays excluded); 2-second and 6-second views; views at 25/50/75/100% of length; average play time per video view = "Average time your video was played per single video view, including any time spent replaying the video." [opened] — [TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/video-play?lang=en)
- Consequence: on TikTok, average watch time includes replay time while views exclude replays, so average watch time above the video's length indicates rewatching/looping. [opened; direct reading of the ads definitions; assumed, not verified, to match creator analytics] — [TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/video-play?lang=en)
- Per-video retention graph: found under "More insights" > Overview > Retention rate, with a line stating "Most viewers stopped watching at..." and the exact second. A drop shows where people left, not why. [search summary of TikTok user-generated "discover" pages, not official documentation] — [TikTok discover page](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/how-to-see-tiktok-retention-rate?lang=en)

[search summary] — [dev.to](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9)

[search summary] [vendor data via eMarketer] — [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/exclusive-data--tiktok-retention-improves-practical--experience-driven-videos-hold-attention)

- Buffer's guide (Oct 2023) calls viewed vs swiped away "one of the most important metrics for ranking in the algorithm"; reads subscribers gained as which content drives loyalty; gives no benchmark numbers. [opened] [vendor] — [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/the-creators-guide-to-youtube-shorts-analytics)
- Metricool advises reading your own retention curve rather than arbitrary benchmarks. [search summary] [vendor] — [Metricool](https://metricool.com/instagram-reel-analytics/)

### Inferences
Mapping metric to dimension. The first column is documented; the "points to" column is my inference plus vendor consensus, not something the platforms state.

| Signal | Where it is | Points to |
|---|---|---|
| Early exit: Shorts "stayed to watch", Reels skip rate (first 3 s), TikTok retention at 1-3 s | all three | Hook: first frame, first line, whether the premise is legible in seconds. Also audience match (who the feed tested it on). |
| Slope of the retention curve in the middle | all three | Pacing and clarity: a steady slope is normal ("always a downward slope"); a cliff at a specific second marks a specific beat (dead air, confusing cut, joke that did not land). |
| Retention at the last seconds / "watched full video" / views at 100% | TikTok, Reels chart, Shorts chart | Ending: whether the punchline arrives before people leave; a drop just before the end suggests the video signals "over" too early (outro, logo, CTA). |
| Average watch time above video length; retention curve above 100% at the start | TikTok (replays included in watch time), Shorts | Rewatch/loop: density, a punchline worth seeing twice, or something missed the first time (could also mean unclear). |
| Sends/shares per reach | Reels (named by Mosseri), all three | "I must show this to someone": relatability and the strength of the joke; matters most for non-follower reach on Instagram. |
| Likes per reach | Reels (named by Mosseri) | Approval from people who already know the series (connected reach). |
| Saves | Reels, TikTok | Utility or rewatch intent; probably weak for pure comedy (no source found). |
| Comments (count and content) | all three | Whether the video provoked a reaction; the text tells which line or character landed. |
| Profile visits, follows per view, subscribers gained | all three | Series pull: does one episode make people want more episodes/characters. |
| Link clicks / bio taps | Reels, TikTok | Brand curiosity; for this series it is the only metric that touches the commercial goal, and it is expected to be tiny per episode. |

- Use rates, not counts: Mosseri's own phrasing is per reach (likes per reach, sends per reach). Counts mostly reflect how many people the feed happened to test the video on.
- Since YouTube's 2025 and 2026 changes, raw Shorts views include first-frame impressions. For cross-episode comparison use engaged views and the stayed-to-watch percentage, not public view counts.
- On Shorts, a video can show a high average percentage viewed while most people swiped away, because that average only counts people who stayed. Always read the two numbers together.
- Compare episodes only against the series' own history and only at similar length (30-40 s is narrow enough to compare directly). No platform benchmark exists.
- None of the platform metrics isolates audio quality or joke clarity; those need the viewer tests in section 7.

### Gaps
- TikTok's official creator-analytics definitions (retention rate, average watch time, watched full video, traffic sources) could not be opened; what is above comes from the ads help center and third-party summaries.
- I did not find an official Instagram Help Center page listing all current Reels insights (saves, shares, profile activity, follows) with definitions; the Instagram material is via Social Media Today quoting Instagram and Mosseri.
- No platform documentation found on how loops appear in the Shorts retention chart (values above 100%), nor a Creator Insider video on reading Shorts retention.
- No independent (non-vendor, methods-disclosed) large-sample study of short-form retention for 2025-2026 was found. No Socialinsider or Buffer 2026 retention study surfaced. The OpusClip "13.5M clips" piece gave no retention numbers in what I saw.
- No evidence found on what saves mean for comedy, or on follows-per-view benchmarks for series content.
- Nothing found that ties a specific metric to audio quality.

## 5. Experiments at small scale

### Takeaway
Native A/B testing does not exist for Shorts (YouTube excludes them explicitly) and I found none for organic TikTok; Instagram's Trial Reels is the one platform tool that lets a small account test a video on non-followers. With heavy-tailed view distributions and a platform-chosen first audience, single-pair comparisons are close to uninformative, so experiments have to be read as patterns across many episodes.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube: "You are not able to A/B test Shorts, Scheduled Lives, and Premiere videos at this time." Test & compare is desktop-only, allows up to 3 titles/thumbnails, and picks the winner by watch time share, not CTR. Tests take a few days to two weeks. [opened] [platform] — [YouTube Help: A/B test titles & thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en)
- YouTube's three outcomes are a model for honest reporting: "Winner" (clearly outperformed, statistically significant), "Performed Same", "Inconclusive." Stated reasons for no winner: variants too similar to matter, or not enough impressions. [opened] [platform] — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en)
- If a video becomes a Short, previously created thumbnail Test & compare access is lost. [search summary] [platform] — [YouTube Help: Test & compare thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714)
- A third-party tool claims it can test Shorts titles/thumbnails; this is not YouTube's native feature. [search summary] [vendor] — [thumbnailtest.com](https://thumbnailtest.com/guides/ab-test-youtube-shorts/)
- Instagram Trial Reels: shown first to non-followers; followers do not see it in feed, Reels tab or profile during the trial; about 24 hours after sharing you see views, likes, comments and shares plus a comparison with your earlier trials; you can "share with everyone," or let Instagram auto-share if it performs well in the first 72 hours. Instagram suggests it for trying a new genre, narrative format or topic. Available to "qualified creators" (criteria not listed). [opened, Portuguese version of the page] [platform] — [Instagram Creators blog](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/instagram-trial-reels)
- Launched December 2024 and expanded to more creators in July 2025. [search summary] — [Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/trial-reels-try-content-non-followers-first/), [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/2025/07/14/instagram-opens-up-trial-reels-feature-to-more-creators/)
- Claim that trial reels can be scheduled natively since April 2026: unverified. [search summary] [vendor] — [Hotmart](https://hotmart.com/en/blog/instagram-trial-reels)
- A worked example of how thin creator "experiments" usually are: Kapwing's trial-reels "study" used two reels, each posted twice (four posts, one account). Reel 1: 2,258 views as trial vs 949 as regular, likes equal at 16. Reel 2: 802 vs 836 views, 0 vs 11 likes. Content type was confounded with the comparison, no statistics were run, and the article's conclusions go beyond what was measured. [opened] [vendor] — [Kapwing](https://www.kapwing.com/resources/instagram-trial-reels-study-what-we-learned-after-a-trial-reel-hit-1-million-views/)
- Heavy tails: a study of online content found that within each set of videos the distribution of weekly views is heavy-tailed. [search summary] [independent, academic] (2011, pre-2023) — [Univ. of Saskatchewan paper](https://www.cs.usask.ca/faculty/eager/performance11.pdf)
- A/B testing leans on the Central Limit Theorem, and with heavy-tailed data convergence is very slow; in production tests at ShareChat and Moj, test statistics often deviated from the theoretical normal distribution at typical traffic levels. [search summary] [independent, arXiv preprint] — [arXiv 2509.26523](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26523v2)
- Anecdote of spread within one account: views ranged from 18 to 19,100 across 35 clips (about 1,000x). [search summary] [practitioner anecdote] — [Typefully post](https://typefully.com/wono_strategy/why-youtube-metrics-are-poison-to-understand-qsZoOB1)
- Claim that TikTok, not the creator, picks the first small test audience, so two posts can differ because of the batch they landed in. [search summary] [vendor blog, plausible but unverified] — [Smash Balloon](https://smashballoon.com/why-your-tiktok-views-look-random/)

### Inferences
- With three episodes a week and no native split test, the feasible design is sequential: change one element for a block of episodes (for example six, two weeks) and compare the block against the previous block, using medians and rates (stayed-to-watch, skip rate, completion, sends per reach) rather than mean views.
- Borrow YouTube's vocabulary for the log: every test ends as Winner, Same, or Inconclusive, and Inconclusive is the expected result at small volume. Do not adopt a change on the strength of one episode.
- Make variants meaningfully different. YouTube's own stated reason for failed tests is variants "too similar"; small accounts can only detect large effects.
- Rates measured inside the video (early exit, retention at second N) are less noisy than reach, because reach depends on the platform's distribution decision. Hook tests should be judged on early-exit rate, not on views.
- The same episode posted on three platforms is a free three-way replication of each change: a change that improves early-exit on all three is more credible than one that moves a single platform.
- Trial Reels is the only native way found to test a deviation from the recipe without exposing followers who value consistency.
- Cover/thumbnail tests matter little for feed-delivered Shorts and Reels, where the video autoplays; they matter for profile-grid browsing. This is inference; no source found.

### Gaps
- No documentation found of an organic A/B or split-test feature on TikTok (ads have one; not researched here).
- No source gives a defensible minimum sample (number of posts per arm) for creator-level tests; the "few dozen posts per arm" figure in a search summary had no citation behind it.
- No evidence found on the effect size of caption style, cover or length changes at small-account scale.
- Trial Reels eligibility thresholds and current (late 2026) behavior not confirmed from Instagram's Help Center.

## 6. Continuous-improvement methods adapted to creative work

### Takeaway
The two frameworks with clear, citable rules both point to the same routine: after each release pick a small number of well-defined improvements (Scrum's 2017 rule was "at least one"), and practice on a narrow, well-defined target with immediate feedback and repetition (Ericsson). The production literature adds the counterweight: cap polish explicitly, and treat changes to the creative recipe as scope changes rather than routine fixes.

### Cited Findings
- Deliberate practice criteria (Ericsson, Psychological Research, published online 2020): (1) "The task must be well defined with a clear goal and be fully understood by the participant"; (2) the participant can perform the task independently; (3) immediate, actionable feedback on each performance; (4) repetition of the same or similar tasks; (5) "The practice task must be designed and performed in accordance with individualized instruction and guidance of a teacher." [opened] [independent, academic] — [Ericsson 2020, PMC8049893](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8049893/)
- Practice meeting the first four criteria but without a teacher is "purposeful practice," not deliberate practice. [opened] — [Ericsson 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8049893/)
- The size of the practice effect is disputed: Macnamara et al. (2014) argued most variance in performance reflects factors other than practice; Ericsson replied that they used a broader definition ("engagement in structured activities created specifically to improve performance in a domain") than his, and Ericsson & Harwell (2019) reported practice explaining over 50% of variance after correction. [opened] — [Ericsson 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8049893/)
- Original paper: Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, Psychological Review 100(3), 1993, pp. 363-406, including the criterion that subjects "should receive immediate informative feedback and knowledge of results of their performance." [search summary] (1993, pre-2023) — [Ericsson 2020, quoting the 1993 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8049893/)
- Scrum Guide, November 2017 update: the Sprint Backlog was to include at least one high-priority process improvement identified in the previous Retrospective. [search summary] — [Scrum.org](https://www.scrum.org/node/13568)
- Scrum Guide 2020 softened this to a suggestion: the most impactful improvements are addressed as soon as possible and may be added to the next Sprint Backlog. The Retrospective's stated purpose is to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness. [search summary] — [Scrum.org](https://www.scrum.org/node/51310), [Scrum Guide 2020 PDF](https://scrumguides.org/docs/scrumguide/v2020/2020-Scrum-Guide-US.pdf)
- "One improvement, or kaizen" the team can implement right away (Scrum Inc.); agree on at least one measurable process improvement for the next sprint (Atlassian); retrospectives fail when improvements disappear after the meeting (Mountain Goat Software). [search summary] [practitioner/vendor] — [Scrum Inc.](https://www.scruminc.com/?p=5134), [Atlassian](https://community.atlassian.com/learning/lesson/how-to-improve-sprint-retrospective), [Mountain Goat Software](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/Agile/scrum/meetings/sprint-retrospective)
- Warnings against over-polishing, from production sources: "pursuing perfection may cost more than the value it delivers" once 3-5% of shots remain in a third retake cycle; creative changes are "scope changes, not routine retakes." [opened] [vendor] — [CG-Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show)
- Warning on rigid cadence: "We probably stuck to that weekly schedule a little more strictly than we needed to, so we needed a break." [opened] — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/an-oral-history-of-homestar-runner-the-internets-favor-1791519879)
- Postmortem principle attributed to Catmull: measuring the outcome without evaluating the process is deceiving; look for what went right for reasons not understood. [search summary, secondary] — [SKMurphy](https://skmurphy.com/?p=141)
- Daily publishing as practice: "I'm kind of sharpening my scales every single day." [opened] — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2022/08/11/youtube-millionaires-nutshell-animations/)

### Inferences
- A solo, AI-assisted production cannot meet Ericsson's fifth criterion (a teacher), so by his own definition this is purposeful practice. The four achievable criteria still give the shape of the routine: one well-defined target per episode ("the first line must state the conflict"), a feedback signal that arrives fast (early-exit rate and retention at 24-48 h, plus a pre-publish viewer test), and repetition of the same target over several episodes before moving on.
- "At least one improvement per release" plus the small-sample limits in section 5 argue for exactly one deliberate change per episode or per block. One change keeps cause and effect readable and protects what the audience already recognizes.
- Split the retrospective output in two lists: fixes to the pipeline (templates, prompts, rigs, checks: bank them, they compound and the audience never sees a discontinuity) and changes to the creative recipe (tone, structure, look, voices: scope changes, test in blocks, or on Trial Reels).
- Catmull's point about process vs. outcome matters with noisy metrics: an episode that flopped on views may have had no process defect, and an episode that spiked may hide one. Review the episode against the rubric before looking at the numbers.
- Set the polish cap in advance (time-box or a "no more than N notes fixed per episode" rule), mirroring the 3-5% rule and DeepBlueInk's fixed weekly output budget.

### Gaps
- No primary kaizen source (Imai, Toyota) was retrieved; kaizen appears here only through Scrum practitioners' use of the word.
- No empirical study found on continuous-improvement routines in creative or animation teams specifically.
- No evidence found (as opposed to opinion) that series audiences punish visible changes between episodes; the consistency warning rests on the project's own premise and on general practice, not on data from this research pass.
- Scrum Guide wording was not read from the PDFs directly.

## 7. Audience feedback and direct viewer tests

### Takeaway
Analytics show where viewers leave, not why; the creators who described their process got the "why" from comments and from noticing which unplanned moments people responded to. Direct tests (five-second recall, muted viewing, watching someone watch) are borrowed from UX practice; the evidence that they work is from interface testing, and the sound-off statistics commonly quoted for social video are old.

### Cited Findings
- A retention drop "doesn't automatically tell you why someone left, but it can point you toward a specific moment worth reviewing." [search summary of a TikTok user-generated page] — [TikTok discover page](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/how-to-see-tiktok-retention-rate?lang=en)
- Comments as cross-platform diagnosis: "You can see amongst the comments what's working for one side, what's not working for the other." [opened] — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2022/08/11/youtube-millionaires-nutshell-animations/)
- Audience response reshaped Homestar Runner: the moments between the planned competitions turned out funnier than the competitions. Viewer emails were the feedback channel. [opened] — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/an-oral-history-of-homestar-runner-the-internets-favor-1791519879)
- Five-second test: a participant sees a single screen for five seconds, then answers recall and impression questions. The term is credited to Christine Perfetti in the mid-2000s. [search summary] — [Smashing Magazine, Dec 2023](https://smashingmagazine.com/2023/12/five-second-testing-case-study), [LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/step-by-step-guide-five-second-testing-ux)
- A 2016 Journal of Usability Studies paper tested the method and reported that most usability problems in its study were found after 5 seconds of exposure; the method measures first reactions and perceived usability, not real use. [search summary] [independent, academic] (2016, pre-2023) — [JUS, Gronier 2016](https://uxpajournal.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/pdf/JUS_Gronier_Nov2016.pdf)
- Sound-off viewing, all dated: Verizon/Publicis survey reported 92% view videos with sound off on mobile and that 50% said captions matter because they watch with sound off; Digiday reported up to 85% of Facebook video views without sound; Sharethrough found 75% say they often watch on mute. [search summary] (2016-2019, pre-2023; Facebook-feed era, self-reported, not TikTok/Reels/Shorts) — [Kapwing roundup](https://kapwing.com/resources/subtitle-statistics), [Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/), [Digiday/Sharethrough](https://digiday.com/media/75-percent-of-people-watch-mobile-videos-on-mute)
- Meta reported captions increased video ad view time by an average of 12%. [search summary] (older Meta ads study, pre-2023) — [3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/studies-find-captions-improve-engagement)
- Critique framing that applies to any viewer test: judge against what the maker intended, and report problems as what a viewer without the maker's context cannot read. [opened] — [11 Second Club Helpful Hints](https://www.11secondclub.com/helpful_hints/the_compliment_sandwich)
- Review order that protects the reviewer's attention: clear small defects before the screening so the judge can "focus on the narrative." [opened] — [CineMontage](https://cinemontage.org/how-the-burger-is-made-the-workflow-secrets-of-bobs-burgers/)

### Inferences
- Adapted tests for a 30-40 s episode, each aimed at something analytics cannot isolate:
  - Five-second test: show the first 3-5 seconds to someone, stop, ask "who is this, where are they, what is about to go wrong?" If they cannot answer, the hook is a clarity problem, not a pacing problem. Matches the platforms' own 3-second skip window.
  - Muted pass: watch once with sound off. Checks whether captions, staging and acting carry the joke. Sound-off share on vertical feeds in 2026 is unknown, so treat this as a clarity test, not as proof that most viewers are muted.
  - Audio-only pass: listen without looking. Checks voice intelligibility, mix and comic timing; this is the only cheap test for audio, which no platform metric isolates.
  - Watch someone watch, saying nothing: note where they smile, where their eyes leave the screen, whether they replay. One naive viewer per episode is cheap; rotate viewers because each sees the series fresh only once.
- Ask test viewers Braintrust-style questions (what was confusing, what did you expect to happen, what would you tell a friend it was about), not "did you like it" and not "how would you fix it."
- Code comments rather than count them: quoted lines, named characters, questions that reveal confusion, requests for more. Quoted lines mark what to keep; confusion marks clarity defects.
- Read comments together with the retention cliff: the second where the curve drops locates the beat, the comments and viewer test explain it.

### Gaps
- No documented method found for testing short-form video with live viewers (the five-second test evidence is for web pages; applying it to video hooks is my adaptation).
- No current (2023-2026) measurement of sound-off viewing on TikTok, Reels or Shorts found; all figures are 2016-2019 surveys from the Facebook autoplay era.
- No research found on the reliability of comments as a quality signal (commenters are a small, self-selected share of viewers).
- "Watching someone watch" has no source in this pass; it is standard usability practice but I did not retrieve a citation for it.
