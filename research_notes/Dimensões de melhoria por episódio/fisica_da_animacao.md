Conventions used in these notes:

- Fetched pages were read through an automated summarizer, so quoted strings are short and should be re-verified before being printed as verbatim quotes.
- "Cited Findings" = what sources state. "Inferences" = my own reasoning about applying it to this project (one swapped full-body image per pose, whole-image transforms, 30 fps). Numbers in Inferences that are not followed by a link are my arithmetic, not sourced.

## 1. The 12 principles (Thomas and Johnston): what each is, and which matter most for weight and comedy

### Takeaway
All twelve are defined consistently across sources; for weight the load-bearing ones are timing/spacing, slow in/slow out, squash and stretch with constant volume, anticipation, and follow-through/overlap, and for comedy the load-bearing ones are staging, exaggeration, anticipation and timing. Nine of the twelve can be expressed with whole-image transforms; solid drawing and appeal live in the drawings themselves.

### Cited Findings
- **Squash and stretch**: gives objects "a sense of weight and flexibility"; the rule is that volume does not change, so lengthening means narrowing — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles of animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Anticipation**: prepares the audience for an action and makes it read as more realistic (dancer bending knees before a jump; a glance off-screen before someone enters) — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Staging**: Johnston and Thomas define it as "the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably clear"; done with placement, light/shadow, camera angle, and removing unneeded detail — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Straight ahead vs pose to pose**: pose to pose draws key poses first and fills in between, and suits dramatic or emotional scenes; straight ahead is more fluid — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Follow through, overlapping action, drag, moving hold**: loose parts keep moving after the body stops and settle back toward the centre of mass; parts move at different rates; drag = parts lag "a few frames" behind; a moving hold adds slight motion (e.g. breathing) to a still character so it does not go "lifeless" — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Slow in and slow out**: more drawings near the beginning and end of an action, fewer in the middle; this emphasises the extreme poses — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Arcs**: natural action follows arched trajectories; thrown objects follow parabolas; mechanical motion is the straight-line exception; faster objects flatten their arcs — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Secondary action**: supporting movement that emphasises rather than competes with the main action; during a dramatic move it is better placed at the start and end than in the middle — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Timing**: "the number of drawings or frames for a given action"; correct timing makes objects appear to obey physics (lighter objects react faster than heavy ones) and also carries mood and personality — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Exaggeration**: strictly realistic motion looks static and dull; the Disney version stays true to reality but in "a wilder, more extreme form"; use restraint so one element does not overwhelm the rest — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Solid drawing**: forms with volume, weight, balance; avoid "twins" (mirrored left and right sides look lifeless). **Appeal**: charisma; simple, readable designs help — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- **Timing vs spacing** (attributed to Richard Williams by a university course blog): timing is where the ball hits the ground (the "boing"); spacing is how the drawings cluster, overlapping at the slow top of the arc and spreading apart as the ball drops faster — [Ulster University course blog](https://blogs.ulster.ac.uk/scottmoore/2023/11/12/animation-studio-workshops-week-7) [search summary]
- Timing and spacing together communicate an object's weight, material, size and speed — [Ulster University course blog](https://blogs.ulster.ac.uk/scottmoore/2023/11/12/animation-studio-workshops-week-7) [search summary]
- Squash that is too extreme or spans too many frames makes a ball look like it is "willing itself to jump"; the squash must be bracketed so it does not begin before impact and is gone on rebound — [Blender 3D by Example excerpt (O'Reilly)](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/blender-3d-by/9781789612561/a3824793-b63e-450d-a5cd-908a3d285b3e.xhtml) [search summary]
- A SIGGRAPH education demo contrasts three balls: constant speed without squash, constant speed with squash, and slow in/out with squash; only the last combines both — [SIGGRAPH HyperGraph](https://education.siggraph.hosting.acm.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/principles/bouncing_ball_example_of_slow_in_out.htm) [opened]
- In Remotion, `posterize: 3` on an interpolation makes a value change "only every three frames", a stepped look (the code equivalent of animating on threes) — [Remotion, Animating properties](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/animating-properties) [opened]

### Inferences
- Mapping to the available controls (whole-image translate/rotate/scale, pose swap, mouth, blink, camera, props, shadow):
  - Timing/spacing and slow in/out: every transform's frame count and easing curve. Fully available.
  - Squash and stretch: non-uniform scale with the anchor at the feet. Constant volume in 2D means scaleX = 1 / scaleY. The current impact squash is scaleY 0.80 with scaleX 1.14 (product 0.91, so the character loses about 9% of its area); area-preserving would be scaleX 1.25. The walking stretch (1.07 x 0.93 = 0.995) already preserves it.
  - Anticipation: a short counter-move before a pose swap or a move (dip and squash before a hop, lean back before a lean forward, shrink before a pop).
  - Follow-through/overlap: no limbs, so it has to be faked by delaying one transform channel against another on the same image (rotation lagging translation, scale settling after position) and by delaying props, shadow and camera against the body.
  - Arcs: thrown props and hops as parabolas (constant horizontal speed, quadratic vertical).
  - Secondary action: blink, breath, bob while talking, listener tilt. Per the cited rule, keep these out of the middle of a big move.
  - Staging: camera framing and which character is allowed to move. One idea at a time means listeners should go nearly still while a gag pose lands.
  - Exaggeration: overshoot amounts, squash depth, hang time, hold length.
  - Solid drawing and appeal: only fixable in the pose drawings (asymmetric poses to avoid "twins", strong silhouettes).
- Pose to pose is the native mode of this project: each pose image is a key. The quality lever is therefore (a) how extreme and readable each key is and (b) the 4 to 10 frames around each swap.
- Rendering characters with stepped values (Remotion `posterize`, or rounding the frame to twos) while the camera stays on ones is a cheap way to get a hand-animated feel; this is my suggestion, not something a source recommends for this case.

## 2. Weight and force: heavy versus light, falls, bounces, hang time

### Takeaway
Weight is read almost entirely from timing and spacing: gravity gives every falling object the same timing, so "heavy" comes from how slowly something starts and stops, how little it bounces, and how hard the world reacts. Falls follow exact spacing rules (Odd Rule 1:3:5:7, or "a quarter of the distance at half the time"), which in code is simply a t-squared ease-in.

### Cited Findings
- **Odd Rule** (Alejandro Garcia, physicist who consulted for animators): under a constant force such as gravity, frame-to-frame spacings measured from the apex go 1, 3, 5, 7, 9... — [Animator Island, Physics in Animation](https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3322) [opened]
- **Fourth Down at Half Time**: the breakdown pose halfway in time between apex and landing sits only a quarter of the distance down from the apex — [Animator Island, Physics in Animation](https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3322) [opened]
- A thrown object keeps even horizontal spacing while its vertical spacing follows the Odd Rule, which produces the parabolic arc — [Animator Island, Physics in Animation](https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3322) [opened]
- All objects fall with the same timing because gravity's acceleration is the same for all of them — [Animator Island, Physics in Animation](https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3322) [opened]
- Real fall distances: 1 foot (30 cm) in 1/4 second; 4 feet (120 cm) in about 1/2 second; 16 feet (almost 5 m) in 1 second — [Animator Island, Physics in Animation](https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3322) [opened]
- Timing sets perceived scale: a character that drops off a roof in 12 frames at 24 fps reads as small because that timing implies a 4-foot house; a 20% change in timing can make a 6-foot character read as about 4 feet — [Animator Island, Physics in Animation](https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3322) [opened]
- Lighter objects react faster than heavy ones; this is the timing principle's link to weight — [Wikipedia, Twelve basic principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation) [opened]
- Each bounce gets shorter as the ball loses momentum — [bouncing-ball tutorial results, e.g. BMCC Adobe Animate tutorial](https://openlab.bmcc.cuny.edu/mmp260-1301-s2019/adobe-animate-bouncing-ball-tutorial) [search summary]
- One university assignment budgets about 160 frames for a light ball's full bounce sequence and about 50 frames for a heavy ball's, i.e. the heavy ball stops bouncing roughly three times sooner — [University of Washington CSE458 project](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse458/16au/content/projects/project6_animation_b) [search summary; single assignment, not a general rule]
- Squash is keyed at impact and stretch a couple of frames before and after each bounce — [BMCC Adobe Animate tutorial](https://openlab.bmcc.cuny.edu/mmp260-1301-s2019/adobe-animate-bouncing-ball-tutorial) [search summary]
- Carnegie Mellon's animation course teaches weight with a heavy-ball/light-ball exercise built on timing, spacing and ease in/out (the page gives no numbers) — [CMU Dopesheet, Lesson Two](https://dopesheet.andrew.cmu.edu/resources/lesson-two-understanding-weight-with-a-heavy-ball-light-ball) [opened]
- Tex Avery's rule for a falling anvil: start the motion only four or five frames before the impact so the gag reads as sudden — [Cartoon Brew, history of wild takes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/a-thrilling-tour-through-the-history-of-wild-takes-in-animation-243744.html) [opened]
- Game "hit stop" guidance: freeze roughly 2 to 4 frames for light hits and 6 to 10 frames for heavy hits — [gamineai.com pixel-art combat guide](https://gamineai.com/blog/pixel-art-combat-fx-hitstop-smear-frames-impact-timing-2026) [search summary; game design source, not cartoon practice]
- Impact frames typically last 1 to 3 frames and smear frames 1 to 2 frames — [80.lv on impact frames](https://80.lv/articles/learn-how-to-add-accents-to-your-animations-with-impact-frames) / [Smear frame, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smear_frame) [search summary; I could not tell which of the listed pages gave the figure]

### Inferences
- A fall coded as `progress = t^2` (the project's prop drop already does this over 8 frames) is exactly the Odd Rule and Fourth Down at Half Time. A linear or ease-in-out fall will read as floaty or mechanical. The rise of a hop or toss is the mirror: `1 - (1 - t)^2`.
- Real-gravity reference at 30 fps (converted from Garcia's figures): 30 cm takes 7 to 8 frames, 120 cm takes 15 frames. The current prop falls the height of a head-plus-hover in 8 frames, which is faster than real gravity for a human-scale drop. That is consistent with Avery's "four or five frames before impact" taste (5 to 6 frames at 30 fps); it reads as a gag, not as weight. To make one particular prop feel heavy, do not slow the fall; change what happens at and after impact.
- Because fall timing is the same for everything, heavy versus light has to be signalled elsewhere:
  - Heavy: zero or one tiny bounce, long impact hold (6 to 10 frames at 30 fps, borrowing the hit-stop range), deep squash on whatever it lands on, strong camera shake, floor shadow that snaps to full size, slow start if a character pushes or lifts it.
  - Light: two or three decaying bounces, drift or rotation in the air, little or no reaction from the character it hits, weak or no shake.
- Bounce decay: sources only say each bounce is shorter. Physically, if each bounce keeps a fraction r of the previous height, the bounce's duration scales by sqrt(r). With r = 0.5, bounce durations go 100%, 71%, 50%; with r = 0.25 (heavy, dead object) they go 100%, 50%, 25%. This is my derivation from constant-acceleration physics, not a quoted animation rule.
- Hang time at the top of an arc comes free from the spacing rule: under the Odd Rule the object covers only 1/N^2 of the distance in the first frame after the apex, so half of the airtime is spent in the top quarter of the height. Cartoon hang (section 3) is an extra hold added on top of that.
- A character landing from a hop should squash on the first contact frame (not before), hold 1 to 2 frames, then recover with a small overshoot stretch. The current landing squash decays as exp(-t/4), which has no stretch overshoot on recovery; a slightly underdamped spring would add one.
- The floor shadow is the cheapest weight cue available: shrink and lighten it with height, and let it reach full size exactly on the contact frame. The code already shrinks it with lift.

## 3. Cartoon physics: the comedic "laws", takes, holds, smears

### Takeaway
Cartoon physics is ordinary physics with the timing bent for the joke: gravity waits for awareness, shock launches bodies upward, and the audience needs a readable beat (about five film frames by Avery's rule) before and after a fast change. A take has a fixed skeleton (start, anticipation, accent, settle) with published starter timings of roughly 4 + 7 + 7 frames at 24 fps, or a held anticipation and a one-frame pop for the Warner style.

### Cited Findings
- Mark O'Donnell's "Laws of Cartoon Motion" (Esquire, June 1980; reprinted by IEEE in 1994), as listed by Wikipedia: (1) a body suspended in space stays there until made aware of its situation; (2) a body passing through solid matter leaves a perforation in its own outline; (3) a falling priceless object takes at least as long to fall as its rescuer needs to run down the stairs, and the rescue fails; (4) fear negates gravity; (5) psychic force (a shock or spooky noise) launches a body straight up; (6) feet need not touch the ground when fleeing; (7) as speed increases, objects can be in several places at once; (8) painted tunnels admit some bodies and not others; (9) cartoon cats can be flattened or sliced but reinflate or snap back — [Wikipedia, Cartoon physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon_physics) [opened]
- A line attributed to Disney animator Art Babbitt: "Animation follows the laws of physics, unless it is funnier otherwise" (Wikipedia presents it as attributed, not verified) — [Wikipedia, Cartoon physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon_physics) [opened]
- Walt Disney discussed the "plausible impossible" on the Disneyland TV programme in 1956; in "High Diving Hare" (1948) Bugs Bunny says he never studied the law of gravity; Roger Rabbit can slip the handcuffs only "when it was funny" — [Wikipedia, Cartoon physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon_physics) [opened]
- Roger Ebert's observation: live-action copies of cartoon physics often fail because realistic injury makes them unfunny — [Wikipedia, Cartoon physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon_physics) [opened]
- Chuck Jones's Road Runner rules (from "Chuck Amuck", as transcribed by Austin Kleon): Rule 8, "Whenever possible, gravity should be made the coyote's greatest enemy"; Rule 9, the coyote "is always more humiliated than harmed by his failures"; Rule 2, only his own ineptitude or Acme products can harm him — [Austin Kleon, Rules of the Road Runner](https://austinkleon.com/2005/09/09/rules-of-the-roadrunner/) [opened]
- Chuck Jones reportedly said the difference between a laugh and no laugh could be one frame out of 24 — [search results on Road Runner timing](https://reactormag.com/zoom-and-bored-transports-the-road-runner-and-coyote-to-a-realm-of-funhouse-physics/) [search summary; source page not identified with certainty]
- A wild take is a reaction that goes beyond realism (bulging eyes, rubber jaw); its suddenness is what makes it pop — [Cartoon Brew, history of wild takes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/a-thrilling-tour-through-the-history-of-wild-takes-in-animation-243744.html) [opened]
- Tex Avery believed the eye registers an action in about five frames (at 24 fps, about a fifth of a second). Comparing his "Of Fox and Hounds" (1940) with "Out-Foxed" (1949): the early take is slow, the later one is instantaneous — [Cartoon Brew, history of wild takes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/a-thrilling-tour-through-the-history-of-wild-takes-in-animation-243744.html) [opened]
- Jim Tyer's "shrink take": the character briefly shrinks before the expression, as the build-up — [Cartoon Brew, history of wild takes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/a-thrilling-tour-through-the-history-of-wild-takes-in-animation-243744.html) [opened]
- Limited animation can make takes better: Pat Ventura's work is cited as showing that sharp timing and extreme poses turn missing in-betweens into an asset — [Cartoon Brew, history of wild takes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/a-thrilling-tour-through-the-history-of-wild-takes-in-animation-243744.html) [opened]
- TV executives once banned them; a CBS memo reportedly said the networks would not accept "any wild takes on characters" — [Cartoon Brew, history of wild takes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/a-thrilling-tour-through-the-history-of-wild-takes-in-animation-243744.html) [opened]
- **Take structure** (School of Motion, for After Effects character animation): four poses. Start; Anticipation (a smaller move opposite to the coming action); Accent (the most exaggerated version, "felt more than seen"); Settle (a less extreme accent that reads as the new state). Built pose to pose — [School of Motion, How to animate character takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- **Basic take timing at 24 fps**: ease into the anticipation over 4 frames (about 90% ease in), go to the accent in 7 frames with linear keys, then 7 frames into the settle with about 70% ease in — [School of Motion, takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- **"Warner Brothers" variant**: hold the anticipation longer (6 frames), then pop to the accent in a single frame with no in-betweens, then 7 frames easing into the settle — [School of Motion, takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- **Anticipation to the anticipation**: add a 12-frame move toward the stimulus before the 4-frame anticipation — [School of Motion, takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- **Double take**: start, anticipation (head turns away, 4 frames), two extra head-turn poses 3 frames apart (back, then away again), accent (9 frames, linear), settle (7 frames) — [School of Motion, takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- **Holding the accent as a moving hold**: alternate between the accent and a slightly less extreme copy at least 4 times, 2 frames apart, linear keys, to make a vibration; works better for negative emotions such as fear or anger — [School of Motion, takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- The same anticipation-accent-settle skeleton scales down to blinks, head turns and mood changes: dip the head and close the eyes as the anticipation, pop slightly past the new pose, settle — [School of Motion, takes](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes) [opened]
- Blog frame analyses of Avery: one take held nine frames with anticipation poses before it; in "Big City Red Riding Hood" Avery does not freeze the take, small parts keep moving during the hold — [Tralfaz, "What Lion?"](https://tralfaz.blogspot.com/2023/08/what-lion.html) and [Tralfaz, Big City Red Riding Hood](https://tralfaz.blogspot.com/2024/08/big-city-red-riding-hood.html) [search summary]
- An 11-frame hold before a cut in Jones's "To Beep or Not to Beep" (1963), a hold before a catapult gag, not a fall — [Tralfaz, Catapulting to Failure](https://tralfaz.blogspot.com/2024/10/catapulting-to-failure.html) [search summary]
- Smears: in "The Dover Boys" (1942) characters stand still or move so fast they blur; a burst of speed is shown by only a frame or two of smeared image between two extreme poses — [Wikipedia, Limited animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_animation) [opened] and [The Dover Boys, Wikiwand](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/The_Dover_Boys) [search summary]
- John Kricfalusi on Dover Boys: smears are only the way to get between poses; "If you don't have great poses to get to, the smears are wasted" — [John K Stuff, Smears and Poses](https://johnkstuff.blogspot.com/2009/07/smears-and-poses.html) [opened]

### Inferences
- Take timings converted to 30 fps (my arithmetic from the School of Motion numbers):
  - Basic: 5 frames into anticipation, 9 frames to accent, 9 frames to settle.
  - Warner pop: 5 frames into anticipation, hold 7 to 8 frames, swap to accent on a single frame, 9 frames to settle.
  - Double take: 5 frames away, two beats 4 frames apart, 11 frames to accent, 9 to settle.
  - Vibrating hold: alternate every 2 to 3 frames, at least 4 cycles (about 10 to 20 frames).
- With one image per pose, a take is built like this: keep the current pose and apply the anticipation as a transform (squash down 5 to 10%, shrink, or lean away) for 5 to 8 frames; swap to the "chocado" image on one frame at an exaggerated transform (stretch tall, scale up, small hop: this is the accent); ease or spring down to the image's rest transform over about 9 frames (the settle). The pose swap lands on the accent frame, so the extreme transform hides the pop. Tyer's shrink take is this exact recipe with scale as the anticipation.
- The current speaker "pop" starts at scale 0.94 and springs to 1.0. That is an entrance overshoot, not an anticipation: there is no counter-move held before the change. Adding a held dip in the previous pose before the swap would turn it into a real antic.
- Avery's five-frame rule becomes about 6 frames at 30 fps (0.2 s). Use it as the minimum hold for anything the viewer must read: an anticipation pose, a prop hanging overhead, a reaction pose. The current 13-frame hang (0.43 s) is about double that minimum, which leaves room for the victim to look up.
- "Gravity waits until you look down" maps directly onto the existing hang-then-fall prop: hang, a beat of awareness (victim swaps to the shocked pose or looks at camera), then the t-squared fall. The law says the awareness beat is what triggers the fall, so the pose swap should come 4 to 6 frames before the fall starts, not at the same time.
- "Psychic force" (shock launches a body upward) is a ready-made transform for shocked characters: a straight vertical pop with hang at the top, shadow shrinking, then fall and squash.
- Antic before a zip: for a fast exit, lean or slide a few pixels opposite to the exit direction for 4 to 6 frames, then leave in 3 to 5 frames with horizontal stretch. A one- or two-frame smear can be faked on a whole image with a strong horizontal stretch plus slight skew or motion blur on the frames in transit.
- Impact hold: freeze prop and victim together for 2 to 4 frames on contact (hit-stop range from section 2) before the squash recovery and bounce-off. Sources for this in cartoons specifically are thin; see Gaps.
- Jones's Rule 9 ("more humiliated than harmed") and Ebert's point match the series' tone: impacts should squash and snap back (Law 9), never linger on damage.

## 4. Limited animation: getting life from few drawings

### Takeaway
Limited animation works when the few poses are strong and funny and something small keeps moving during the holds (mouth, head, blink, a cycle), with the camera and cuts doing the rest. Sources agree the failure mode is not "few drawings" but weak poses and dead holds.

### Cited Findings
- Hanna-Barbera did not invent held drawings, background pans and walk cycles; it relied on them more heavily than theatrical studios because it had less time and money — [Yowp, The Hanna-Barbera Tricks](https://yowpyowp.blogspot.com/2018/06/the-hanna-barbera-tricks.html) [opened]
- Concrete Yogi Bear examples: an establishing shot that is only a background with a cross-fade; a slow camera pan over a background where the only animation is a cycle of one lower leg going up and down; dialogue where the characters stay on one held cel while only the mouth changes — [Yowp, The Hanna-Barbera Tricks](https://yowpyowp.blogspot.com/2018/06/the-hanna-barbera-tricks.html) [opened]
- A Carlo Vinci walk used eight drawings, each exposed for two frames, with the background sliding; his "realisation/shock take" for Boo Boo is singled out as what made early shows lively — [Yowp, The Hanna-Barbera Tricks](https://yowpyowp.blogspot.com/2018/06/the-hanna-barbera-tricks.html) [opened]
- A commenter's summary of the studio approach: "held expressions with very limited animation" kept the cartoons from feeling static — [Yowp, The Hanna-Barbera Tricks](https://yowpyowp.blogspot.com/2018/06/the-hanna-barbera-tricks.html) [opened]
- Some 1958 Huckleberry Hound cartoons had characters that "simply popped from pose to pose without any fluidity" — [Yowp, Mr. Jinks vs. Dog](https://yowpyowp.blogspot.com/2024/04/mr-jinks-vs-dog.html) [search summary]
- Hanna-Barbera's limited animation suited small TV screens and close-up, dialogue-driven humour; Jay Ward offset very limited animation with satire and deadpan writing; in anime the convention is mouths moving with occasional blinks — [Wikipedia, Limited animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_animation) [opened]

## 5. Motion design physics in code: springs versus easing, parameters, overlap, procedural secondary motion

### Takeaway
Springs give overshoot and settle for free and keep velocity continuous, so they suit arrivals, pops and settles; easing curves suit motions with a known duration and no rebound (falls as ease-in, slides to a stop as ease-out). Published defaults cluster around stiffness 100 to 210 with damping 10 to 26 at mass 1, and Apple's guidance caps "bounce" at about 0.4 for UI, which is a useful ceiling for everything except deliberate gags.

### Cited Findings
- **Remotion `spring()` defaults**: mass 1, damping 10, stiffness 100, overshootClamping false, from 0, to 1 — [Remotion, spring()](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/spring) [opened]
- Remotion's descriptions: mass is "the weight of the spring. If you reduce the mass, the animation becomes faster"; damping is how hard the animation decelerates; stiffness affects "how bouncy your animation is"; `overshootClamping: true` caps the value at `to` — [Remotion, spring()](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/spring) [opened]
- `durationInFrames` stretches the spring curve to an exact length; `delay` holds the initial value for N frames; `reverse` plays it backwards; order of application is duration, then reverse, then delay. Passing `frame - 20` also delays the start. `measureSpring()` exists to compute a spring's length — [Remotion, spring()](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/spring) [opened]
- **Remotion Easing**: `quad` (t^2), `cubic` (t^3), `poly(n)`, `sin`, `circle`, `exp`, `ease`, `back(s)` (pulls back slightly as it starts), `bounce`, `elastic(bounciness)` (default 1 overshoots a little once; 0 does not overshoot; N overshoots about N times), `bezier(x1,y1,x2,y2)` (same as CSS cubic-bezier), plus `Easing.in`, `Easing.out`, `Easing.inOut` wrappers — [Remotion, Easing](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/easing) [opened]
- Remotion animations must be driven by `useCurrentFrame()`; CSS transitions cause flicker in renders. Examples clamp with `extrapolateLeft/Right: "clamp"` and express durations as multiples of fps — [Remotion, Animating properties](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/animating-properties) [opened]
- **Why springs** (Apple, WWDC23 "Animate with springs"): good animation keeps position and velocity continuous; linear motion has "a sharp corner and a jump in velocity"; springs are the only animation type that stays continuous both from rest and from an initial velocity, and they come to rest gradually — [Apple, WWDC23 session 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/) [opened]
- **Apple's bounce scale**: 0 is a smooth gradual change and the recommended default when unsure; about 0.15 "doesn't feel very bouncy yet" but is brisker; 0.3 is where bounciness becomes noticeable; be cautious above about 0.4 because it "may feel too exaggerated for a UI element". Example pairs used in the session: duration 0.5 s with bounce 0, 0.15, 0.3; duration 0.6 s with bounce 0.2 — [Apple, WWDC23 session 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/) [opened]
- Apple's advice: choose duration first for pacing, then bounce for character; add bounce for playfulness or when the motion should feel physical; a spring's settling duration is unpredictable, so sequence on the perceptual duration instead — [Apple, WWDC23 session 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/) [opened]
- Apple's conversion: mass = 1, stiffness = (2*pi / duration)^2, with a damping formula that depends on bounce. A developer-forum thread says the published damping equation is wrong — [Apple, WWDC23 session 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/) [opened]; disputed by [Apple Developer Forums thread 739811](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/739811) [search summary]
- UIKit damping ratio: 1 decelerates smoothly with no oscillation; values closer to 0 oscillate more — [Apple, UIView spring animation docs](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/uikit/uiview/animate(withduration:delay:usingspringwithdamping:initialspringvelocity:options:animations:completion:).md) [search summary]
- **Motion (Framer Motion) spring options**: damping default 10 (0 oscillates forever), mass default 1 (higher is "more lethargic"), bounce default 0.25 on a scale where 0 is critically damped and 1 is extremely bouncy, duration default 0.3 s, restSpeed 0.1, restDelta 0.01; setting stiffness/damping/mass overrides bounce/duration. Stagger helper: `stagger(0.1)` delays each child by 0.1 s, from first, last, centre or edges — [Motion docs, transitions](https://motion.dev/docs/react-transitions) [opened]
- **React Spring presets** (tension/friction at mass 1): default 170/26, gentle 120/14, wobbly 180/12, stiff 210/20, slow 280/60, molasses 280/120; `clamp: true` stops at the target with no bounce — [React Spring, config](https://www.react-spring.dev/docs/advanced/config) [opened]
- **After Effects overshoot/bounce expression** (a decaying sine added after a keyframe), commonly shared starting values: Dan Ebberts's version uses freq 3 (oscillations per second) and decay 5; other tutorials use freq 4 / decay 2, or amp 0.1 / freq 2 / decay 2; higher decay stops sooner — [Motion Array, common AE expressions](https://motionarray.com/blog/6-common-after-effects-expressions-you-should-be-using), [Tuts+, inertial bounce](https://photography.tutsplus.com/tutorials/how-to-animate-inertial-bounce-expressions-in-after-effects--cms-41469), [Creative Cow thread](https://creativecow.net/forums/thread/expressions-bounce-version-of-this-overshoot-expre/) [search summary]
- **Overlap by offset**: School of Motion builds follow-through by copying the root's rotation keys to each successive part and shifting each copy later by 1 frame (2 frames for a softer result), scaling the copied motion down, and letting the tip overshoot slightly; a small dip before the move serves as anticipation — [School of Motion, follow-through in After Effects](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/animation-tricks-follow-through-after-effects) [opened]
- Animation Mentor's advice on curves: do not let tangents go completely flat, a small slope keeps secondary motion alive — [Animation Mentor blog](https://www.animationmentor.com/?p=87279) [search summary; the page itself returned 403]

### Inferences
- Damping ratio is the single number that predicts overshoot: zeta = damping / (2 * sqrt(stiffness * mass)), and first-overshoot fraction = exp(-pi * zeta / sqrt(1 - zeta^2)). This is textbook second-order-system maths, applied on the assumption that Remotion's spring is a standard mass-spring-damper; I did not verify Remotion's integrator. Results:

  | Config (mass 1) | zeta | First overshoot |
  |---|---|---|
  | Remotion default, damping 10 / stiffness 100 | 0.50 | about 16% |
  | React Spring default 170/26 | 1.00 | none |
  | React Spring gentle 120/14 | 0.64 | about 7% |
  | React Spring stiff 210/20 | 0.69 | about 5% |
  | React Spring wobbly 180/12 | 0.45 | about 21% |
  | Project camera arrive, 22/170 | 0.84 | about 1% |
  | Project camera to drop, 20/160 | 0.79 | about 2% |
  | Project listener attention, 14/120 | 0.64 | about 7% |
  | Project default-stiffness grow, 11/100 | 0.55 | about 13% |
  | Project overlay grow, 12/140 | 0.51 | about 16% |
  | Project card up, 10/170 and 9/150 | 0.38, 0.37 | about 28% |
  | Project speaker pop, 9/200 | 0.32 | about 35% |
  | Project effect pop, 8/220 | 0.27 | about 41% |

- If Apple's bounce maps to damping ratio as zeta = 1 - bounce (this is what the commonly cited form of Apple's formula implies; the fetched summary of the formula was garbled and the formula is disputed, so treat as approximate), then Apple's "noticeable" 0.3 is zeta 0.7 (about 5% overshoot) and the "cautious above" 0.4 is zeta 0.6 (about 10%). By that yardstick the project's character and overlay pops (28 to 41% overshoot) are far past UI taste. That is defensible for comedy accents, but note the speaker pop's overshoot applies to only a 6% scale change, so the visible overshoot is about 2% of body height; the effect pop is the one that visibly rings.
- A practical ladder for this series, derived from the table and the Apple/React Spring values (my recommendation): camera zeta 0.8 to 1.0 (no visible bounce); characters settling into a pose zeta 0.5 to 0.7; gag accents and props zeta 0.3 to 0.45; anything below 0.3 rings for many cycles and reads as jelly.
- Mass as the weight dial: Remotion says lower mass is faster. For a heavy prop or character raise mass (slower start, slower ring) and raise damping with it so the overshoot does not grow; for a light one lower mass. Stiffness then sets snappiness independent of weight.
- Easing by intent: fall = `Easing.in(Easing.quad)` (exactly the Odd Rule); rise against gravity or slide to a stop = `Easing.out(Easing.quad)` or `out(cubic)`; a move with anticipation built in = `Easing.in(Easing.back(s))` or `Easing.bezier` with a negative first y; a hit that should not rebound = spring with `overshootClamping: true` or React Spring-style clamp; arrival with rebound = underdamped spring. Linear only for mechanical objects (per the arcs principle, mechanical motion is the exception).
- `Easing.bounce` gives a canned decaying ground bounce for props without simulating anything; pair it with a squash on each contact and a shadow that scales with height.
- Overlap with only whole-image transforms (my adaptation of the School of Motion offset trick):
  - Drive rotation from the same spring as translation but with `delay` of 2 to 3 frames and lower damping, so the body arrives, then the lean arrives and rocks back.
  - Drag: tilt the image opposite to its horizontal velocity while it moves (top lags the feet when rotation is anchored at the feet), then let the tilt overshoot through zero when it stops. Velocity is available in code as position(frame) minus position(frame - 1). The walk already adds a fixed lean; making it proportional to velocity gives the stop its follow-through.
  - Stagger channels on a pose change: translate first, scale settle 2 frames later, rotation 2 frames after that. One to two frames per link is the sourced starting offset at 24 fps.
  - A held prop, hat or sign that exists as a separate image can follow the character through a spring on the character's position (a one-pole lag), which is true follow-through at the cost of one extra layer.
  - Shadow and dust respond a frame or two after contact; the camera responds after the character (section 6).
- The decaying-sine "inertial bounce" from After Effects ports directly: value += amp * sin(2*pi*freq*t) * exp(-decay*t), with t in seconds after the key. With freq 3 and decay 5 the wobble is under 10% of its start after about 0.46 s (14 frames at 30 fps) and under 1% after about 0.92 s (my arithmetic). It is an alternative to a spring when you want to choose the frequency and length directly.
- Use `durationInFrames` on a spring when a settle has to finish before the next line starts; Apple's point about unpredictable settling time applies to cut timing too.

## 6. Camera physics: follow with lag, overshoot, shake decay, drift, whip-pan anticipation

### Takeaway
A good 2D camera eases toward its subject instead of being pinned to it, leads into the direction of action, and ignores small movements; impact shake is best modelled as a "trauma" value that decays linearly while the visible shake scales with its square or cube and is driven by smooth noise, with rotation used sparingly.

### Cited Findings
- Pinning the camera to the character gives a result that is "jittery, jumpy, short-sighted, and boringly static"; instead have the camera ease between its current position and the character's position, so it does not respond immediately to small shifts — [GMTK, How to Make a Good 2D Camera](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera) [opened]
- Slow damping lets the subject drift ahead of the camera and reduces look-ahead room, so offset the camera ahead of the subject in the direction of travel and glide between offsets when direction changes — [GMTK, 2D Camera](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera) [opened]
- Dead zone: a window inside which the subject can move without the camera reacting; do not move the camera on every jump (it causes nausea); treat horizontal and vertical movement differently; place the subject low in frame to leave room above — [GMTK, 2D Camera](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera) [opened]
- Framing two points of interest: move the camera to a point between them, or zoom to keep both visible — [GMTK, 2D Camera](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera) [opened]
- "Juice": shake for explosions and impacts, tuned in both intensity and direction (Celeste shakes along the dash direction); hit stop freezes the action for a split second while camera and particles keep going (Katana Zero); a brief zoom-in for big moments (Dave the Diver); fast shaking is troublesome for some viewers — [GMTK, 2D Camera](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/how-to-make-a-good-2d-camera) [opened]
- **Trauma shake model** (Godot recipe emulating a GDC talk): trauma is clamped to 0..1 and impacts add to it; trauma decays linearly at 0.8 per second; shake amount = trauma^2 (use power 2 or 3); offset = max_offset * amount * noise, with max_offset (100, 75) px; rotation = max_roll * amount * noise with max_roll 0.1 rad ("use sparingly"); smooth noise (OpenSimplex, period 4, 2 octaves) with different seeds per channel instead of raw random — [KidsCanCode, Godot screen shake](https://kidscancode.org/godot_recipes/3.x/2d/screen_shake/) [opened]
- The talk being emulated is Squirrel Eiserloh's GDC 2016 "Math for Game Programmers: Juicing Your Cameras With Math" — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023146/Math-for-Game-Programmers-Juicing) [search summary; the Godot page itself does not name the speaker]
- Bevy's implementation of the same model uses a decay of 0.5 per second (full trauma gone in 2 s) and an exponent of 2 because shake that rises linearly does not feel punchy — [Bevy, 2D screen shake example](https://bevy.org/examples/camera/2d-screen-shake/) [search summary]
- A human camera operator anticipates: predicts the actor's next move and pans that way, sometimes overshooting and getting slightly ahead of the subject — [Reallusion forum, realistic camera look-at rig](https://forum.reallusion.com/PrintTopic376589.aspx) [search summary; forum post]
- Springs suit motion that must pick up from an existing velocity without a jump, which is the case when a camera is redirected mid-move — [Apple, WWDC23 session 10158](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/) [opened]

### Inferences
- The project's current shake is 24 px * exp(-t/5 frames) on two fixed sines at 2.3 and 3.1 rad/frame, translation only. Compared with the trauma model: (a) the exponential tail never quite ends, whereas linear trauma decay with a squared response falls off fast near the end and stops cleanly; (b) fixed sines trace a regular Lissajous loop, while per-channel noise looks less mechanical (Remotion ships a deterministic noise package, or two or three incommensurate sines per axis approximate it); (c) a little roll adds a lot, but 0.1 rad (5.7 degrees) is the game-scale maximum; for a 9:16 talking-heads frame I would start near 0.5 to 1 degree. The model also composes: a second impact adds trauma instead of restarting the animation.
- Shake length from the sourced decay rates: trauma 1.0 at 0.8/s lasts 1.25 s (about 37 frames at 30 fps) in total, but because of the square, visible shake is below a quarter of peak after half that time. For a gag impact in a 30 to 40 second episode, an initial trauma of 0.4 to 0.6 gives roughly 0.5 to 0.75 s of shake (my arithmetic).
- Shake direction should match the hit: a prop landing from above is mostly vertical, with the first displacement downward (the direction of the blow), then noise.
- Heavy versus light (section 2) can be expressed purely through trauma added: light prop 0.2, heavy prop 0.6 or more plus a 2 to 4 frame hit stop during which the camera still moves, as in the Katana Zero example.
- Follow with lag: the camera springs are near critical (zeta about 0.8), which matches "ease toward the subject, no bounce". Lag can be made explicit by starting the camera move 2 to 4 frames after the new speaker's pop, so the character leads and the camera follows; the reverse (camera leaving first, 2 to 4 frames before a character enters or a prop appears) is the operator-anticipation effect and tells the viewer where to look.
- Whip pan: no source gave numbers. Applying the anticipation and take logic from sections 1 and 3: a small counter-move (1 to 2% of the travel, 3 to 4 frames), a fast travel (4 to 6 frames, with horizontal motion blur or stretch acting as the smear), then a slightly underdamped settle (zeta about 0.6 to 0.7, one small overshoot). This is my construction.
- Handheld drift: also unsourced numerically. A very low-frequency noise on camera x, y and a hair of rotation serves as the camera's own moving hold; it should be much smaller and slower than character breathing so that the two do not read as one wobble, and it should stop during a hit stop.
- Dead zone applied here: do not move the camera for a character's hop, bob or squash; only pose-level or position-level changes should re-frame. The slow 2.5% push-in already in the code acts as a moving hold for the frame.
- The existing punch-in (zoom starts at 1.1x and springs to 1) on a location change is the "brief zoom for big moments" idea; reserving a stronger version for the episode's main gag keeps it meaningful.
