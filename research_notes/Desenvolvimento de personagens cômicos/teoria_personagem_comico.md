# Craft theory of comic character development (for a fixed cast of four in 30-40 s episodes)

Reading note for the report writer. Source quality varies and is flagged per line:
- **[primary]** = the author's own text or the author speaking in an interview that I fetched and read.
- **[secondary]** = a review, summary or encyclopedia page that I fetched and read; quotes are as that page gives them.
- **[snippet]** = seen only in a search-result summary; the page itself was not opened (or returned 403/402). Treat wording as approximate and do not present as a verbatim quote.

## 1. How do the main teachers define a comic character? (Kaplan, Vorhaus, Egri, McKee, Truby, Johnstone)

### Takeaway
The comedy-specific teachers (Kaplan, Vorhaus) define a comic character by what they lack and how they see: an ordinary person with too few skills, a strong skewed viewpoint, who keeps trying to win and believes every move will work. The general dramatists (Egri, McKee, Truby, Johnstone) supply building tools that still apply: a three-layer profile, choice under pressure as the only real proof of character, a weakness that paralyses, and status as something a character does moment by moment.

### Cited Findings

**Steve Kaplan (The Hidden Tools of Comedy, 2013)**
- Kaplan's "comedy equation": "An ordinary guy or gal struggling against insurmountable odds, without many of the required tools with which to win, yet never giving up hope." The tools are presented as practical outgrowths of this formula. [secondary, review quoting the book] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Non-Hero: "Note that we don't say comic hero, but a non-hero." The non-hero lacks some or many skills but stays determined; he is neither an idiot nor an exaggerated fool. [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Skills dial: "The more skills your character has, the less comic and the more dramatic the character is." This is Kaplan's working distinction between comedy and drama. [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Winning: "Comedy gives the character permission to win." "In winning, there are no 'shoulds.'" The character may do whatever they think will get them what they want, even if it makes them look stupid; they are not trying to be funny. [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- "Doing whatever needs to be done in order to win is what sets a comedic character apart from a dramatic one." and "One of the biggest mistakes a new writer can make is trying to be funny." [secondary, article reporting a Film Courage interview with Kaplan] — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/09/expert-advice-unpacking-your-comedic-screenwriting-toolbox)
- Positive Action: "Every action your character takes, your character actually thinks is going to work," however foolish it looks. The review says this is what softens abrasive characters such as Basil Fawlty and Louie DePalma. [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Active Emotion: "The emotion that is created simply by being in the situation is the exact right emotion to be having." [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Metaphorical Relationship: "beneath every surface relationship is a true, or essential, metaphorical relationship." Examples: couples who behave like business partners, cats and dogs, or babies; Oscar and Felix as an old married couple. [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Straight Line / Wavy Line: Kaplan rejects "funny guy and straight guy"; "The real dynamic is that of watcher and watched." He cites John Cleese: comedy is "watching somebody watch somebody do something silly." The Straight Line creates or is blind to the problem; the Wavy Line struggles with it and mostly watches in confusion. Kramer works because Jerry, George or Elaine watch him in "bemused amazement." [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- The non-hero is "a beat behind" what the audience has already figured out; one summary frames the two tools as: Winning asks "What do your characters want?", Non-Hero asks why your characters know so much. [snippet] — [Powell's listing](https://www.powells.com/book/hidden-tools-of-comedy-the-serious-business-of-being-funny-9781615931408); [Goodreads reader notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/19228619-the-hidden-tools-of-comedy/843770-kmo)
- Kaplan separates "likable" from "sympathetic": a sympathetic character is one the audience can relate to. [secondary, paraphrase] — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/09/expert-advice-unpacking-your-comedic-screenwriting-toolbox)

**John Vorhaus (The Comic Toolbox, 1994)**
- Comic perspective is the character's way of seeing the world; Vorhaus's phrase is a "STRONG comic perspective", and the further it sits from "average" the better. [secondary, a hobbyist blog summarising the book; it misnames the title as "The Comic Toolkit" and hedges with "I think that's the idea"] — [The Duck Webcomics](https://next.theduckwebcomics.com/news/2017/apr/20/comedy-part-two-creating-comedic-characters)
- Exaggeration: "The perspective is to be exaggerated as much as you can." George Costanza's "pettiness" or "selfishness" is not enough until pushed "to the Nth degree"; same for Scrooge's greed. [secondary, same blog; the examples may be the blogger's, not Vorhaus's] — [The Duck Webcomics](https://next.theduckwebcomics.com/news/2017/apr/20/comedy-part-two-creating-comedic-characters)
- In an interview Vorhaus frames comedy as drama plus exaggeration, which lets an audience look safely at the underlying issue. [snippet of a podcast interview] — [Become a Writer Today podcast](https://www.buzzsprout.com/1055644/4054379-how-to-write-comedy-even-if-you-re-not-funny-with-john-vorhaus)

**Lajos Egri (The Art of Dramatic Writing, 1946)**
- Three dimensions of character: physiology, sociology, psychology. Egri calls the categories that fill them in the character's "bone structure." [snippet] — [Goodreads blog: Story and the Dimensions of Character](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6058330-story-and-the-dimensions-of-character?tab=author)
- Physiology (sex, age, build, posture, appearance, defects, heredity) shapes outlook: Egri's example is the sick man who values health above all while a healthy person rarely thinks of it. [snippet] — [Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6058330-story-and-the-dimensions-of-character?tab=author)
- Sociology covers class, occupation, education, home life, religion, community standing, hobbies. Psychology (moral standards, ambition, frustrations, temperament, attitude to life, complexes, abilities) is "the product of the other two." Item lists differ slightly between summaries. [snippet] — [Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6058330-story-and-the-dimensions-of-character?tab=author); [Goodreads quotes page](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/102268)

**Robert McKee (Story, 1997)**
- "CHARACTERIZATION is the sum of all observable qualities of a human being, everything knowable through careful scrutiny." "This singular assemblage of traits is CHARACTERIZATION... but it is not CHARACTER." [primary, authorised excerpt] — [Writers Store excerpt, part one](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)
- "True CHARACTER is revealed in the choices a human being makes under pressure - the greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation, the truer the choice to the character's essential nature." "Choices made when nothing is at risk mean little." "As he chooses, he is." [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)
- Example: an undocumented domestic worker in a rusted station wagon and a neurosurgeon in a Porsche (opposite characterizations) both reach a burning school bus; what each chooses shows who they are. [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)
- Contrast between surface and core sustains a long-running character: Bond's "lounge lizard" exterior hides "a thinking man's Rambo." Rambo's sequels, where surface and core match, give him "less dimension than a Saturday morning cartoon." [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)
- "Minor roles may or may not need hidden dimensions, but principals must be written in depth." [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)
- The excerpt does not discuss comic characters. — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)

**John Truby (The Anatomy of Story, 2007)**
- Truby treats establishing Weakness and Need as the most critical step, and the first one. A hero with a need is "paralyzed in some way at the beginning of the story by his weakness." Desire is a goal outside the character, the external track. Truby sometimes uses "problem" and "weakness" interchangeably, which confuses readers. [snippet] — [Stage 32: Understanding Character Want and Need](https://www.stage32.com/blog/2782)

**Keith Johnstone (Impro, 1979)**
- "Status is a confusing term unless it's understood as something one does. You may be low in social status, but play high, and vice versa." [secondary, book summary with quotes] — [James Clear's notes on Impro](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- "A comedian is someone paid to lower his own or other people's status." Tragedy "also works on the see-saw principle: its subject is the ousting of a high-status animal from the pack." [secondary] — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- "Moment by moment each person adjusts his status up or down a fraction." "We're all giving status signals, and exchanging subliminal status challenges all the time." [secondary] — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- "A good play is one which ingeniously displays and reverses the status between the characters." [secondary] — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- Playable status signals: holding eye contact reads as powerful, breaking it and glancing back reads as feeble; a still head while speaking raises status; a hesitant "er" at the start of a sentence lowers it; the "fear-crouch" is low, the open "cherub posture" is high; the most secure high-status person is the most relaxed. [secondary] — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- Master-servant: "a master-servant scene is one in which both parties act as if all the space belonged to the master." "A servant's primary function is to elevate the status of the master." [secondary] — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- A pratfall is funny when the character loses status and the audience does not sympathise; when a very high-status person falls, the audience feels itself rise a step. [secondary] — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- People have a preferred status (they like being high or low) and manoeuvre to get back to it. [snippet] — [Improwiki: Status](https://improwiki.com/en/wiki/status)
- The book has four sections (Status, Spontaneity, Narrative Skills, Masks and Trance) and grew out of Johnstone's teaching and his work at the Royal Court Theatre. [secondary] — [Wikipedia: Impro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impro:_Improvisation_and_the_Theatre)

### Inferences
- The teachers stack rather than compete. Egri gives the dossier (what is true of the person), Vorhaus the lens (how they see), Kaplan the engine (they want to win, lack the skills, think each move will work), McKee the proof (what they choose when it costs something), Johnstone the staging (who is up, who is down, right now).
- For the four-person cast, each role can be written as a Kaplan sentence: "wants X, lacks skill Y, never gives up." A gossip who lacks discretion, an optimist who lacks any sense of risk, a bureaucrat who lacks flexibility, a rival who lacks self-awareness. This is my application, not a source claim.
- Kaplan's skills dial is a practical tuning knob: if a character solves the problem competently, the beat turns dramatic. Give competence only where a sincere moment is wanted.
- Watcher/watched (Kaplan) and the status see-saw (Johnstone) both say a gag needs two people: one doing, one reacting. In a 30-40 s episode the reaction shot is probably the cheapest laugh available.
- Johnstone's signals (eye contact, head stillness, posture, hesitation) translate directly to limited animation: status can be drawn with a pose swap and no dialogue.
- The bureaucrat is a natural "plays high, is socially low" figure and the rival a "plays high, keeps being lowered" figure; the optimist can play low and still win. Assigning each a preferred status gives a default and a clear thing to reverse.

### Gaps
- Vorhaus on **flaws** and **humanity**: no source I could open covers them. The assignment brief names his four elements as comic perspective, exaggeration, flaws, humanity, and my recollection of the book agrees (flaws create "comic distance" so the audience can laugh; humanity makes the audience care), but I could not confirm this online. Check the book before quoting.
- Kaplan's exact wording comes through a review; the Backstage article by Kaplan himself returned 403. The term "comic perspective" belongs to Vorhaus; the Kaplan review I read does not use it.
- Truby: no direct quotation retrieved. Whether he distinguishes "psychological need" from "moral need" (I recall that he does) is unverified here.
- Egri: only secondary summaries; exact category lists should be checked in the book.
- Johnstone: the phrase "status transactions" appears in the summary; the "see-saw" wording is quoted there too, but page references are missing.
- None of the general dramatists (Egri, McKee, Truby) was found addressing comedy specifically.

## 2. Want vs need vs flaw vs wound, and how a comic character differs from a dramatic one

### Takeaway
In the standard model the want is an external goal the character knows about, the need is the internal lack they discover, the flaw/weakness is what blocks them, and the wound/ghost is the past event that produced it. The comedy teachers keep the want and the flaw and mostly drop the healing: the comic character pursues the want with too few skills and complete conviction, and the flaw stays.

### Cited Findings
- Want = "an external goal they desire to achieve", plot-driven, tangible, known from the start. Need = "what's lacking internally — their feelings and flaws they need to address", theme-driven, discovered during the story. Wounds "heal" when the character realises the need. Examples: Shrek wants to be left alone in his swamp and needs to accept friendship and vulnerability; Rocky wants to go the distance and needs to believe in his own worth. [secondary] — [No Film School, Mayank Shandilya, 8 Jan 2026](https://nofilmschool.com/character-want-vs-need)
- Ghost / Lie / Need model: the Ghost is the past event(s) that shaped the character; the Lie is the false belief that grows from it; the Need is the truth they must realise to heal the wound; the character typically chases the Want instead of the Need. [snippet] — [Helping Writers Become Authors: the character's ghost](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-4/)
- Truby: weakness and need come first; the weakness paralyses the hero at the start; desire is the outside goal. [snippet] — [Stage 32](https://www.stage32.com/blog/2782)
- Dissent: John August calls want-vs-need "complete and utter bullshit", says separating them is "generally frustrating and almost universally pointless", and notes he can find wants and needs in finished films but that this does not make the framework useful for planning. He replaces it with one question: "Why is the character doing what he's doing?", which scales from a single scene to a whole film and demands visible action. [primary; opinion] — [John August, "Rethinking motivation", 25 Mar 2008](https://johnaugust.com/?p=1000)
- Comedy vs drama, per Kaplan: the difference is skills and the licence to win. "The more skills your character has, the less comic and the more dramatic the character is." "Doing whatever needs to be done in order to win is what sets a comedic character apart from a dramatic one." [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/09/expert-advice-unpacking-your-comedic-screenwriting-toolbox)
- Kaplan allows growth inside a feature: in romantic comedy the clumsy non-hero becomes more sensitive and mature in the romantic moments (Bill Murray in Groundhog Day). [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Kaplan (as reported) says screenwriters often mishandle the journey: the destination is the external goal, and the journey is where the character sees the internal goal mattered more (forgiving someone, letting go of fear, letting someone in). The article notes he presents these as tendencies, not rules. [secondary, paraphrase] — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/09/expert-advice-unpacking-your-comedic-screenwriting-toolbox)
- Vorhaus (interview): comedy is drama with exaggeration added. [snippet] — [Become a Writer Today podcast](https://www.buzzsprout.com/1055644/4054379-how-to-write-comedy-even-if-you-re-not-funny-with-john-vorhaus)
- McKee: pressure reveals character; it need not change it. Bond's core is revealed film after film and the series survives on the surface/core contrast. [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)

### Inferences
- Working definitions for a serial comedy (my synthesis): **want** = what the character is trying to win in this episode; **flaw** = the permanent skill gap or skewed view that makes them go about it wrongly; **need** = the thing that would fix them, which the series withholds; **wound** = optional backstory that explains the flaw and can be revealed once, late, as depth.
- A dramatic arc closes the gap between want and need. A sitcom keeps the gap open and renews the want every episode. That follows from combining the status quo material in section 3 with Kaplan's "never giving up hope"; no single source states it this way.
- August's single question ("why is the character doing what he's doing?") is the better fit for a 30-40 s script: there is room for one visible motive, not for a want/need pair.
- In a 30-40 s episode the want should be stated or shown in the first line, because the want is the only part of the model the audience needs to follow the gag.

### Gaps
- I found no named teacher stating outright that "comic characters keep the flaw and never meet the need." It is a reasonable inference and common craft talk, but treat it as inference.
- No source found that applies ghost/wound specifically to sitcom characters.
- "Flaw" and "wound" have no agreed definitions across teachers; the terms above come from popular craft sites, not from the six named books.

## 3. Do sitcom characters change? Status quo, flat vs round, and depth without change

### Takeaway
The dominant sitcom convention is that characters do not fundamentally change; Seinfeld made it an explicit rule. Forster's theory explains why this works: flat characters are instantly recognised, easily remembered and "best when they are comic". Depth comes from discovery and revelation (new facets found by writers and actors, contradictions between surface and core), with slow evolution early in a run while the show finds the character.

### Cited Findings

**Status quo**
- Larry David's rule for Seinfeld was "no hugging, no learning": avoid sentimentality and moral lessons; characters never learn or grow from their wrongdoing. [snippet] — [IMDb trivia](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0098904/trivia); [Looper](https://looper.com/869993/the-two-dark-rules-larry-david-had-on-the-set-of-seinfeld)
- The motto ran against 1980s-90s broadcast norms shaped by sentimental family sitcoms; the Curb Your Enthusiasm finale applied the same idea to David's own character. [snippet] — [Time](https://time.com/6964642/curb-your-enthusiasm-finale-seinfeld)
- Larry David has reportedly said he has never seen anybody change, as his reason for disliking character growth in comedy. [snippet; URL looked unusual and was not opened, so verify before using] — [Westword](https://www.westword.com/?p=40627618)
- Counterpoint: despite the motto, the Seinfeld friendships "have real lessons to offer". [snippet; opinion] — [Oprah.com](https://www.oprah.com/relationships/What-Sitcoms-Have-Taught-Us-About-Friendship)
- Dan Harmon's story circle ends with "Return" to the familiar situation and "Change" (having changed). [snippet] — [Reedsy on the story circle](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/dan-harmon-story-circle/)
- A critic's reading of Community ("Basic Story") is that the characters are stuck in a sitcom purgatory and certain changes cannot happen while they remain sitcom characters. This is the reviewer's view, not Harmon's words. [snippet; opinion] — [A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/community-basic-story-1798180104)

**Flat vs round (E. M. Forster, Aspects of the Novel, 1927)**
- Flat characters "were called 'humours' in the seventeenth century"; "In their purest form, they are constructed round a single idea or quality." "The really flat character can be expressed in one sentence", e.g. Mrs Micawber: "I never will desert Mr. Micawber." [primary] — [Aspects of the Novel, ch. 4, Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4)
- "One great advantage of flat characters is that they are easily recognized whenever they come in." "A second advantage is that they are easily remembered by the reader afterwards." [primary] — [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4)
- "They are best when they are comic. A serious or tragic flat character is apt to be a bore." [primary] — [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4)
- "Dickens' people are nearly all flat... Nearly everyone can be summed up in a sentence, and yet there is this wonderful feeling of human depth." [primary] — [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4)
- "The test of a round character is whether it is capable of surprising in a convincing way. If it never surprises, it is flat." [primary] — [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4)
- Critical defence: flat characters put protagonists in relief (Hamlet needs Polonius and Laertes); Forster himself says "a novel that is at all complex often requires flat people as well as round" and that Dickens's "immense success with type suggests that there may be more in flatness than the severer critics admit." [secondary; essay] — [Aatif Rashid, "In Defense of Flat Characters", Kenyon Review, 27 Aug 2018](https://kenyonreview.org/2018/08/in-defense-of-flat-characters/)

**Depth without change (showrunner testimony)**
- Michael Schur: "the characters are built brick by brick slowly by a large group of people." On comedies the premise often matters more early on. [primary, interview] — [The Ringer, 2 Apr 2020](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Greg Daniels left several Office characters open on purpose: "he very deliberately left them blank at the beginning", to find them "organically" through pitching. Oscar's sexuality "was a thing that got discovered along the way." [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Michael Scott was recalibrated after The 40-Year-Old Virgin: Daniels said "We need to work 20 percent of that guy into this character", which Schur says "literally saved the show." [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Ron Swanson: the first idea (a corrupt politician) was rejected ("No, that's not good") in favour of a "19th-century-libertarian version"; one episode (Ron's hernia) showed the room "that's who he is." [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Leslie Knope: writers first had her "lean into a politician cadence", then followed Amy Poehler's natural delivery; Schur: "she's right and we're wrong." [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Early Leslie was poorly received as too close to Michael Scott; the characters developed over the early seasons as actors and writers worked out what was fun and kept it (Aziz Ansari's account). [snippet] — [Uproxx oral history](https://uproxx.com/?p=852741); [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/parks-rec-amy-poehler-leslie-knope-improvised-change/)
- Relationships as a source of new facets: the plan was for April to become like Leslie's mother; mid-season two Schur began to think April might one day become Leslie, and Leslie learned to use April's abilities without pushing her to copy her. [snippet] — [Showbiz Cheat Sheet](https://www.cheatsheet.com/news/parks-and-recreation-mike-schur-april-ludgate-leslie-never-happened.html)
- McKee's mechanism for depth without change: a contradiction between characterization and true character (Bond) keeps a repeating character interesting; when surface and core coincide the character flattens (Rambo sequels). [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)

### Inferences
- There is a real disagreement. The Seinfeld camp holds characters fixed on principle; the Schur/Daniels camp lets characters evolve and warm up (Michael Scott, Leslie). Both keep the comic engine intact: what changes in the second camp is calibration and affection, not the core perspective.
- Forster gives the test to use: each of the four should be expressible in one sentence (flat, recognisable in a second), and occasionally do something that surprises "in a convincing way" (round). A one-sentence definition plus a rare convincing surprise is the practical recipe for depth without an arc.
- Distinguish **development of the character by the writers** (discovery, early calibration) from **change in the character** (learning a lesson). Short-form serial comedy wants plenty of the first and almost none of the second.
- For 30-40 s episodes with no guaranteed viewing order, status quo is close to a technical requirement: each episode must work for a viewer who has seen none of the others. This is my reasoning, not a source claim.
- Ways to add depth that the sources support: (a) a contradiction between surface and core (McKee); (b) a facet discovered through a new pairing (April/Leslie); (c) a trait found by accident and kept (Oscar, Ron's hernia); (d) borrowing from the performer's natural voice (Poehler), which for animation means the voice actor or TTS voice.

### Gaps
- Harmon's own essay on how TV differs from film (Channel 101 wiki, "Story Structure 105") returned 402 and was not read. I recall that he argues TV characters must return to where they started, but could not verify or quote it.
- No academic study of sitcom character stasis was retrieved; the evidence here is practitioner testimony and criticism.
- No direct Larry David quotation was retrieved from a primary interview.
- Nothing from Tina Fey was found in this pass.
- "Backstory" as a depth technique for sitcoms specifically is not sourced here beyond general fiction advice.

## 4. Flanderization: what it is, why it happens, how writers avoid it

### Takeaway
Flanderization is the gradual inflation of one trait until it swallows the character. The cited causes are long runs, joke repetition and writers reaching for the established gag. Sourced prevention advice is thin: critics describe the disease much better than practitioners describe the cure.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: a process in which a character's essential traits are oversimplified and exaggerated over a serial work, until one element (often originally mild) becomes the "primary defining trait." The term was coined by TV Tropes after Ned Flanders. It has been called the opposite of character development, or "character derailment." [secondary] — [Wikipedia: Flanderization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanderization)
- TV Tropes wording: taking a single (often minor) action or trait and exaggerating it more and more over time until it completely consumes the character. [snippet; TV Tropes returned 403, wording is as quoted by CBR] — [TV Tropes: Flanderization](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Flanderize); [CBR](https://www.cbr.com/flanderization-ned-flanders-synonymous-with-bad-writing/)
- The Flanders case: began as "perhaps the only genuinely well-meaning, good-natured person in Springfield", a contrast to Homer; over time became a fundamentalist whose Christianity was the defining trait; later drifted back toward the original portrayal. [secondary] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanderization)
- More detail: Flanders was "initially set up as a perfect foil for Homer and not much else"; writers then added a contented temperament, friendship with Homer, strained relations with his parents, teetotalism and devout faith; Maude's death added grief and doubt; then "his faith became the only consistent piece of his character, with the other complications and aspects fading away", leaving him as one-note as in season one but "far more unpleasant." [secondary; opinion] — [Brandon Zachary, CBR, 30 Mar 2020](https://www.cbr.com/flanderization-ned-flanders-synonymous-with-bad-writing/)
- Causes: declining writing quality in a long-running franchise, writers short of new ideas falling back on established tropes, repeated jokes, minor characters getting more spotlight; sitcoms are especially prone (Friends and The Office cited). [secondary; opinion] — [CBR](https://www.cbr.com/flanderization-ned-flanders-synonymous-with-bad-writing/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanderization)
- TV Tropes' explanation for sitcoms: character development matters far less than the Rule of Funny. A common side effect is that other personality traits are dropped as one is focused on. [snippet] — [TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Flanderize); [Medium RPG guide quoting TV Tropes](https://medium.com/rpguide/flanderizing-characters-b8d7e30d2d59)
- Building a character around a single quirk tends to cause it (said of tabletop RPG characters). [secondary] — [Wikipedia, citing Loaded Dice 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanderization)
- Other cited examples: Peter and Brian Griffin (Family Guy), Kevin Malone (The Office), SpongeBob, Lisa Simpson (Amelia Tait in Vice calls her "the most Flanderized character in TV history"). [secondary] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanderization)
- It is distinct from early-run adjustment: if the character is simply different at the start before the writers know what to do with them, TV Tropes calls that "Characterization Marches On." [snippet] — [Medium RPG guide](https://medium.com/rpguide/flanderizing-characters-b8d7e30d2d59)
- Not always bad, per TV Tropes; and some shows (Rick and Morty) are said to avoid it consciously. [snippet] — [TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Flanderize); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanderization)
- General anti-flatness advice (not sitcom-specific, not from showrunners): pair each trait with its opposite or a counterweight (a rude jerk who is also charming); do not remind the audience of the trait at every turn. [snippet] — [Goodreads blog: One-Dimensional Characters](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/13465865-most-common-writing-mistakes-pt-51-one-dimensional-characters)
- Practitioner-adjacent safeguards: Schur prefers granular definitions, "a kind of person who has these specific character traits", over copying a type; writers who are "experts" in a character stay on rewrites to keep the voice consistent. [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)

### Inferences
- There is a tension with Vorhaus: he says exaggerate the perspective "as much as you can", and Flanderization is exaggeration gone wrong. The reconciling distinction (my reading): exaggerate the **perspective** (how they see everything) and not a **tic** (one behaviour or catchphrase). A perspective generates new behaviour in new situations; a tic only repeats.
- The Flanders history suggests the mechanism: the character is not ruined when a trait grows but when the **other** traits and relationships stop appearing. A simple audit follows: across the last N episodes, did each character show at least one trait other than the headline one?
- A four-archetype cast in very short episodes is at high risk, because the label (gossip, optimist, bureaucrat, rival) is the fastest joke available. High output (many short episodes) reproduces the "long run" condition quickly.
- Forster's point that flat comic characters are a strength means the goal is not roundness everywhere. The goal is a stable one-sentence core plus a short list of secondary traits that keep getting used.
- McKee's Rambo example is Flanderization described before the term existed: the surface became the whole character.

### Gaps
- I found no showrunner or named writer giving explicit advice on preventing Flanderization. The prevention material above is inference plus general fiction advice.
- The TV Tropes main and Analysis pages returned 403; their text is known only through quotes in other sources.
- The claim that Flanderization is driven by audience response to popular gags (writers repeating what got laughs) is plausible and often said, but unsourced here.

## 5. Character bibles and writers' room tools

### Takeaway
Published guidance on show bibles is generic (backstory, world rules, tone, character breakdowns). The most concrete tools come from Schur's account of the Office and Parks rooms: define characters by granular traits and by negation, keep a fixed principle the character always applies, assign character "experts", and budget story time across the cast.

### Cited Findings
- A series bible starts as a pitch document and, once in production, becomes an evolving reference used inside the writers' room to track character backstories, timeline events, world rules and tone. There is no fixed format; examples run from 31 to 53 pages. [snippet] — [Final Draft: 10 TV show bibles](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/10-tv-show-bibles-every-tv-writer-should-study); [Wikipedia: Bible (screenwriting)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_(screenwriting))
- Definition by negation: for Janet (The Good Place) "we just kept saying what she wasn't" (not "a robot", not "a girl") and left the rest to the actor. [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- A governing principle that predicts the character on any topic: asked about gay marriage, Ron Swanson would say "The government shouldn't tell anybody what to do about anything." [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Granularity over type: describe "a kind of person who has these specific character traits" and avoid defining a character or a pair as a copy of another show's ("instead of saying, 'This is a Jim and Pam relationship'", borrow only "an aspect"). [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Character experts: "Writers find aptitudes for characters"; those writers stay on rewrites to add jokes and protect the voice. [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Story budgeting: Schur's rough Parks rule was 60/30/10 (core characters 60% of the main story, side characters 30%, a flexible 10%). A weak episode often meant a strong character was underused: "we didn't write Ron Swanson into a good story this time." [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- Counter-casting writers: Daniels gave sentimental episodes to writers who disliked sentimental material, to keep them balanced. [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- External pressure as a calibration tool: Schur describes Pawnee's local media as a good source of pressure on Leslie and her team. [snippet] — [Uproxx/Sepinwall: Parks and Recreation history, part 1](https://uproxx.com/sepinwall/parks-and-recreation-history-part-1-perd-joan-and-the-other-pawnee-crazies)
- Pairing and contrast: Flanders was created as a foil for Homer (polite and level-headed against brash and thick-headed). [secondary] — [CBR](https://www.cbr.com/flanderization-ned-flanders-synonymous-with-bad-writing/)
- Pairing through perception: Kaplan's metaphorical relationship (two colleagues who are "really" an old married couple) and straight line / wavy line (one blind to the problem, one watching it). [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Voice: James Cary (sitcom writer, Sitcom Geeks) observes that much of what a character is comes from how they talk, and advises against giving all the punchlines to one character. [snippet] — [British Comedy Guide: James Cary](https://comedy.co.uk/people/james_cary/press/13)
- Egri's bone structure works as a bible template: fill in physiology, sociology and psychology for each character as a starting point for three dimensions. [snippet] — [Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6058330-story-and-the-dimensions-of-character?tab=author)

### Inferences
- A workable one-page character sheet for this series, assembled from the sources (my synthesis): (1) one-sentence definition (Forster); (2) comic perspective, how they read any event (Vorhaus); (3) what they want in general and which skill they lack (Kaplan); (4) governing principle that answers "what would they say about X?" (Schur on Ron); (5) "is not / would never" list (Schur on Janet); (6) preferred status and physical signals (Johnstone); (7) three secondary traits to rotate, as protection against Flanderization; (8) one surface/core contradiction (McKee); (9) voice rules: sentence length, vocabulary, verbal habits (Cary); (10) Egri facts only where they generate jokes.
- "What would X never do" is not documented as a named room tool in anything I read; Schur's definition by negation is the closest sourced equivalent. Present it as a practice inferred from that, not as an industry standard.
- With four characters there are six pairings. A relationship map can assign each pairing a Kaplan metaphor (e.g. teacher/pupil, old married couple, rival siblings) and a default status order, then note which pairings have not been used recently. The metaphors are mine to illustrate; the method is Kaplan's.
- The gossip and the bureaucrat are natural wavy-line watchers of the optimist's straight-line blindness; the rival is the character whose status the format can keep lowering. Rotating who watches whom is a cheap way to vary episodes without changing anyone.
- Since `scripts/write.ts` generates scripts from a prompt, the "character expert" role maps to a fixed character sheet injected in the prompt plus a per-character voice check at review. This is an application note, not a source claim.

### Gaps
- No source describing the actual contents of a sitcom character bible in detail was found; available articles concern pitch bibles for dramas.
- No sourced writers' room account of relationship maps or of "would never" rules.
- Cary's posts were seen only as index snippets; his book Writing That Sitcom would be the place to check.
- Nothing from Tina Fey's Bossypants or 30 Rock room accounts was retrieved.

## 6. Revealing character in very little screen time (one line, one choice, one prop)

### Takeaway
Introduce the character doing something, attach one telling detail or object, and make the first beat a choice, because choice is what reveals character. Forster adds the compression test: if the character fits in one sentence, the audience can recognise them on entry.

### Cited Findings
- A character introduction is most potent when the character is doing something; the action itself can be the introduction. [snippet] — [Final Draft: how to introduce characters](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/how-to-introduce-characters-in-your-script-like-these-iconic-examples)
- One telling detail beats a paragraph: a shirt and tie with rolled-up sleeves (a Sorkin example) communicates working-class solidarity and readiness for action. A protagonist's introduction should also carry some internal tension. [snippet] — [Creative Screenwriting: character introduction tips](https://www.creativescreenwriting.com/cswcms/character-introduction-tips-screenplay/)
- Signature object: ask whether there is an item immediately associated with the character (glasses, a weapon, a particular piece of clothing), and consider a defining line of dialogue. [snippet] — [No Film School: character introductions](https://nofilmschool.com/character-introductions)
- Keep written introductions to a few lines; minor characters do not need full description. [snippet] — [Neil Chase Film](https://neilchasefilm.com/how-to-introduce-characters-in-a-screenplay/)
- Blake Snyder's Save the Cat principle: "The hero has to do something when we meet him so that we like him and want him to win." The blogger quoting it notes it need not be a literal act of kindness. [snippet] — [Goodreads blog: A "Save the Cat" Moment](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/18256148-a-save-the-cat-moment?tab=book)
- Choice as revelation: "True CHARACTER is revealed in the choices a human being makes under pressure"; "Choices made when nothing is at risk mean little." [primary] — [Writers Store excerpt of McKee](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)
- One-sentence character: "The really flat character can be expressed in one sentence"; such characters "are easily recognized whenever they come in." [primary] — [Forster, Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4)
- Reaction as revelation: showing how characters react to chaos is described as a more direct way to reveal personality than a lot of dialogue. [snippet; attribution within the article unclear] — [Chortle](https://www.chortle.co.uk/news/2018/06/16/40246/sitcoms_have_lost_their_nerve_on_grotesque_characters)
- Reaction is half the joke: comedy is "watching somebody watch somebody do something silly" (Cleese, via Kaplan). [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Status reads instantly through the body: eye contact, head stillness, posture, hesitation, use of space. [secondary] — [James Clear on Impro](https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/impro)
- A single line can carry the whole worldview if the character has a governing principle (Ron Swanson's answer to any policy question). [primary] — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/04/02/tv/mike-schur-dwight-schrute-michael-scott-ron-swanson-leslie-knope)
- The correct emotion is the one the situation would really produce, which keeps a short beat legible. [secondary] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)

### Inferences
- The same-event test: put one small event in front of all four and write each reaction in one line. If the lines could be swapped between characters, the perspectives are not yet distinct. This follows from Vorhaus's comic perspective and Kaplan's watcher/watched; it is not a quoted exercise.
- One choice per episode: give the featured character a two-option moment with a small cost (McKee scaled down to seconds). The option they pick is the characterisation; no explanation needed.
- One prop per character, used consistently, does Forster's "recognised whenever they come in" work in the first frame. The project already uses one recurring object (the bateia that glows); each cast member could carry an equivalent. Which props is a design decision, not something the sources prescribe.
- For limited animation, Johnstone's status signals are the cheapest reveal: pose and head position can establish who is up before the first word.
- A first line that states the want ("I just need X before Y") lets Kaplan's Winning do the work; the audience then reads everything else as an attempt to win.
- The guidance on introductions comes from feature screenwriting, where an introduction happens once. In a short-form series every episode is someone's first, so the reveal has to be repeated each time without feeling repeated: same prop and posture, new choice.

### Gaps
- Most findings in this section are search-snippet level from screenwriting education sites; none is a primary practitioner account, and none addresses sub-minute formats.
- No source found on character revelation specific to short-form vertical video or sketch-length animation.
- ScreenCraft and The Script Lab were named as suggested sources but nothing usable from them surfaced.
