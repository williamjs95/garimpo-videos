# Content properties that drive completion, rewatch and sharing in vertical short-form video

Scope: the content side only, not the algorithm. Applied to a 30-40 s animated comedy series (small shop owners in a Brazilian neighborhood arcade).

Evidence-type labels used below:
- **[PEER]** peer-reviewed journal or conference paper
- **[PREPRINT]** arXiv or working paper, not confirmed peer-reviewed
- **[THESIS]** doctoral thesis
- **[PLATFORM]** data published by the platform (or relayed from it)
- **[VENDOR]** analytics or tool vendor data with undisclosed or partly disclosed methods
- **[LORE]** creator or agency blog, no disclosed method
- **[PRE-SF]** published before 2020 (pre-short-form); whether it was tested on short video is noted alongside
- **(opened)** I read the page or abstract myself; **(search summary)** I only saw a search-engine summary or snippet, so treat the details with more caution

Research limits: about 16 tool calls. Several full texts (the Ling et al. PDF, the Essex thesis, PubMed) could not be read, so a number of details come from abstracts or search summaries only. This is flagged per item.

## 1. The psychology of sharing (Berger & Milkman, STEPPS, identity, benign violation) and whether it holds on TikTok/Reels

### Takeaway
The classic finding is that high-arousal emotion (awe, amusement, anger, anxiety) predicts sharing more than valence does. It is pre-short-form and partly shaky: the lab arousal manipulation failed two replications. The emotion-over-information result was confirmed on YouTube video ads, where amusement, surprise, plot and characters raised sharing and early branding lowered it, which matters directly for a comedy with brand winks. On TikTok, the closest replications show that humor and imitating platform templates predict every engagement metric, and that high-arousal or identity-laden content drives shares. "Relatable" content behaves differently: it drives comments rather than shares.

### Cited Findings
- **[PEER][PRE-SF]** Berger & Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*), "What Makes Online Content Viral?": in an analysis of New York Times articles plus experiments, content that evokes high-arousal positive emotion (awe) or high-arousal negative emotion (anger, anxiety) is more viral. Positive content is more viral than negative overall, but activation level, not valence, drives the effect. Not tested on short video by the authors. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf) (search summary); [Northeastern mirror](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf) (search summary)
- **[PEER] Replication failure (2024, *Psychological Science*)**: "Does Physiological Arousal Increase Social Transmission of Information? Two Replications of Berger (2011)". They used the same arousal manipulation, updated the sharing measure to social media sharing, and in both studies found no effect of incidental physiological arousal on undergraduates' willingness to share news articles. Caveat: this targets Berger's 2011 *incidental* arousal study, not the 2012 NYT field result. Arousal produced by the content itself was not directly refuted. — [Psychological Science page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/09567976241257255/) (search summary)
- **[PEER][PRE-SF, but tested on online video ads]** Tellis, MacInnis, Tirunillai & Zhang (2019, *Journal of Marketing*), "What Drives Virality (Sharing) of Online Digital Content? The Critical Role of Information, Emotion, and Brand Prominence". Method: two field studies of YouTube ads coding 11 emotions and 60+ ad characteristics, plus a third study that validated predictions on an independent sample. Findings:
  - Informational content significantly *reduces* sharing, except in risky contexts or for new or unfamiliar products.
  - Amusement, excitement, inspiration and warmth increase sharing.
  - Drama elements (surprise, plot, characters including babies, animals and celebrities) arouse emotion.
  - Showing the brand early (vs late) hurts sharing.
  - Sharing peaks at a moderate length, which for ads was about 1.2-1.7 minutes.
  - The authors note that marketers rarely use the drivers that help sharing and overuse the ones that hurt it.
  - [AMA research insight](https://ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-what-drives-sharing-of-digital-content) (search summary); [AMA classroom page](https://www.ama.org/2020/06/11/what-drives-virality-sharing-of-online-digital-content-the-critical-role-of-information-emotion-and-brand-prominence) (search summary); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/author=3583613) (search summary)
- **[PEER][PRE-SF] Identity signaling**: Berger & Heath (2008, *JPSP* 95(3), 593-607), "Who Drives Divergence?", argues that people adopt or abandon cultural tastes to signal identity, and abandon a signal once other groups co-opt it. It is about products and tastes, not shared media, and was not tested on short video. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Divergence.pdf) (search summary); [Knowledge at Wharton podcast](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/from-cool-to-passe-identity-signaling-and-product-domains/) (search summary)
- **[LORE/expert interview]** Berger: "what we share is a signal of who we are". People share things that make them look good, smart or in the know (social currency). — [Penn Today on #10YearChallenge](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/science-behind-facebooks-viral-10yearchallenge) (search summary); [APA Speaking of Psychology "viral" episode](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/viral) (search summary)
- **[PEER][PRE-SF] Benign violation theory**: McGraw & Warren (2010, *Psychological Science*) say humor arises when something seems wrong (a violation) and at the same time okay (benign). A violation reads as benign when:
  - an alternative norm makes it acceptable;
  - the viewer is only weakly committed to the violated norm; or
  - the viewer is psychologically distant from it.

  — [McGraw & Warren 2010 PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf) (search summary); [APS press release "Immoral behavior is funny, but only if it also seems benign"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/people-think-immoral-behavior-is-funny-but-only-if-it-also-seems-benign.html) (search summary)
- **[PEER]** Warren & McGraw (2016, *JPSP*), "Differentiating What Is Humorous From What Is Not", six studies: humor comes from something that threatens well-being, identity or normative beliefs while seeming okay at the same time. — [PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/warren.mcgraw.2016a.pdf) (search summary); [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jpspy/pdf/10.1037/pspi0000041~differentiating-what-is-humorous-from-what-is-not) (search summary)
- **[PEER]** Warren & McGraw (2016, *Journal of Marketing Behavior* 2(1), 39-67), "When Does Humorous Marketing Hurt Brands?": humor can trigger amusement and negative feelings at once. "Severe" violations can hurt brand attitudes even when they are funny, while "mild" ones are safer for the brand. — [Now Publishers](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/JMB-0027) (search summary); [Eller College news "Those funny ads may make you laugh, maybe not buy"](https://eller.arizona.edu/news/2016/12/those-funny-ads-may-make-you-laugh-maybe-not-buy) (search summary)
- **[PEER] Timing and distance**: in the "Hurricane Sandy" jokes study (McGraw lab), jokes were rated funnier the day before the storm than in the days right after it. Humor depends on psychological distance from the harm. This measured perceived funniness, not sharing. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/time-tragedy-violation-and-humor-psychologists-discover-a-comedic-sweet-spot/) (search summary)
- **[LORE/expert quote]** McGraw: humor is attention-getting and "often facilitates sharing behavior", but a funny PSA may not produce the desired behavior change. — [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/asmagazine-archive/node/1303) (search summary)
- **[THESIS, secondary claim, unverified]** "Over 91% of viral advertising is humorous", quoted second-hand in a UEA thesis. I could not trace the original data. — [UEA thesis](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/95914/1/Marco%20Capasso%20Thesis%20Final%20Version.pdf) (search summary)

### Inferences
- For a comedy series, the arousal finding becomes this rule: aim for strong amusement or surprise (high-arousal positive) rather than mild "smile" humor. The replication failure says incidental arousal does not move sharing, so the emotion has to come from the content itself.
- Tellis et al. map almost one-to-one onto the project's existing rule ("isso parece anúncio? Então corta"). Information content and early or prominent branding are the two measured share-killers. Keeping the Garimpo brand as late, small winks is supported by evidence and is not just a matter of taste.
- Benign violation suggests the best target for a shop-owner comedy is mild, everyday violations: a stingy customer, absurd bureaucracy, a neighbor shop's petty rivalry. They are wrong enough to be funny and harmless enough to share without social risk. Severe violations, which punch down or cruelly mock real groups, risk hurting the brand.

### Gaps
- No replication of Berger & Milkman's *field* result on TikTok/Reels/Shorts specifically was found in this session.
- Berger's STEPPS framework (*Contagious*, 2013) and his 2014 review of word-of-mouth functions (impression management, emotion regulation, information acquisition, social bonding, persuasion) were not opened or located with a URL in this session. Check them before citing.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that directly measures "tag a friend" or "this is so me" sharing on short video.

## 2. Studies specifically on TikTok/Reels/Shorts virality (with sample sizes)

### Takeaway
Academic samples are small (hundreds to about 1,000 videos), and most are domain-specific (science, politics, health). The consistent signals:
- Humor and imitating platform templates or formats predict views, likes, comments and shares.
- Shot scale (close-up or medium), on-screen text and point of view matter visually.
- Creator follower count dominates prediction, which content cannot control.
- Likes, comments and shares respond to different things and should not be pooled.

### Cited Findings
- **[PREPRINT] Ling, Blackburn, De Cristofaro & Stringhini (2021)**, "Slapping Cats, Bopping Heads, and Oreo Shakes: Understanding Indicators of Virality in TikTok Short Videos" (also deposited at NSF PAR, which suggests it was later published, possibly at ACM WebSci 2022; venue not confirmed). Method: N = 400 TikTok videos, collected and hand-labeled, with a Random Forest classifier. The search summary reports AUC 0.86, which was not visible on the abstract page. Findings:
  - Follower count is the most powerful predictor.
  - Close-up and medium shot scales "play an essential role".
  - Video lifespan, presence of text and point of view are also influential.
  - The abstract does not state the direction of each effect.
  - Shares and views are not available for every video, and prior work found that more shares do not imply more views.
  - [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02452) (opened); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02452) (search summary; I could not extract text); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10399977) (search summary)
- **[PEER] Bunquin & Segrè Cohen (2026, *Public Understanding of Science*)**, "When scientists 'Tok: Memetic communication and scientific information engagement in short video-based social media". Method: 1,025 science videos from 113 TikTok creators, content analysis with multilevel models. Findings:
  - **Humor** and **imitation** (using recognizable platform templates or trends) positively predicted likes, shares, comments **and** views.
  - **Reappropriation** (remixing other media content) negatively predicted engagement.
  - The authors stress aligning with platform-specific vernacular.

  Effect sizes were not visible. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42533308/) (search summary; the page itself was blocked); [SSRC MediaWell](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/?p=517519) (search summary)
- **[THESIS]** Bunquin's dissertation "Funny, Felt, Familiar" names three consistent engagement predictors: remixing other media, humor, and imitation of social-media storytelling templates and styles. Note that its result on remixing has the *opposite* sign to the paper's reappropriation result above, which I could not reconcile from snippets. — [U. Oregon Scholars' Bank](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu//bitstreams/088eb9ba-85fb-41a9-a039-89ed688295c2/download) (search summary)
- **[THESIS] Bai (Essex, 2026)**, "TikTok Comedy During COVID-19: A Multimodal Study". Method: 741 comedic TikTok videos from 14 creators, data collected in 2023-April 2024, testing how multimodal, linguistic and platform features jointly predict three engagement outcomes. Finding: it argues explicitly that treating likes, comments and shares as interchangeable proxies is a "questionable assumption". I could not open the full text (SSL error, and my second download attempt was denied), so the per-feature results are unknown. — [Essex repository PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/43953/1/phd_thesis_bai_20261005.pdf) (search summary)
- **[PREPRINT] TikTok, 2025 German federal election.** Method: engagement modeled per view; the figures come from appendix Table S7. Findings:
  - Relatability: medium relatability gave **+3.7% comments/view but −11.3% shares**; high relatability gave **+8.0% comments but −7.4% shares**. The authors suggest relatable content invites self-expression in the comments rather than passing the video on.
  - Moralized and high-arousal content (outgroup animosity, identity language, anger, disgust) were the strongest predictors of active engagement. Outgroup animosity gave +20.6% comments and +5.0% shares.
  - The content is political, not comedy.

  — [arXiv 2509.10336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.10336) (search summary); [PsyPost "Negativity drives engagement on political TikTok"](https://www.psypost.org/negativity-drives-engagement-on-political-tiktok/) (search summary)
- **[PEER] Teen mental-health TikTok content (2024, PMC)**: the categories with the highest engagement rates were relatable media representation (26.01%), health campaign (25.82%), social isolation (25.69%), **humor (25.65%)** and eating disorders (24.09%). The top categories differ only marginally. — [PMC11753871](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11753871/) (search summary)
- **[PEER] SciTePress (2025), TikTok news videos (N = 1,000)**: the comedy category led in both popularity and frequency. — [SciTePress PDF](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2025/138346/138346.pdf) (search summary)
- **[PEER] Spanish creators (2024)**: TikTok humor is mainly affiliative humor that turns everyday situations into comedy (case study of 5 creators, qualitative). — [ResearchGate, "El humor en TikTok"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379758820_El_humor_en_TikTok_un_caso_de_estudio_de_cinco_creadores_de_contenido_espanoles) (search summary)
- **[PEER, algorithm-side context] Klug et al. (WebSci '21)**, "Trick and Please": in a sample of 300,617 trending TikTok videos, they tested whether engagement (e.g. shares) drives promotion. Relevant only as a reminder that the share-to-views link is not one-to-one. — [ACM DL PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3447535.3462512) (search summary)
- **[PEER] Methods caveat**: a JMIR-indexed paper argues that TikTok content analyses must control for follower count, because raw engagement mostly reflects audience size. — [PMC10865194](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10865194/) (search summary)
- **[PREPRINT] Johns et al. (2026)**, "When Jokes Cross the Line: Analyzing Regular Humor and Dark Humor in YouTube Shorts". Dataset "TwistedHumor": 1,211 Shorts and 33,041 comments. Findings:
  - Regular humor is associated with more positive comment sentiment.
  - Dark humor draws more mixed, neutral and sometimes more toxic reactions.
  - Dark humor clusters around critique, coping, awkwardness and identity expression.
  - No view, share or like comparisons appear in the abstract.

  — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00046) (opened)

### Inferences
- Design for **shares** and **comments** as separate goals:
  - "This is so me" relatability fills the comments.
  - Shares need a stronger hook of amusement or surprise, or a "you have to see this" moment.
  - A good episode probably needs both a relatable situation and an escalating, surprising payoff.
- "Imitation" in Bunquin's sense means using recognizable TikTok formats: POV framing, "type of person" structures, trending sound patterns. That counts in favor of borrowing native format grammar even in animation.
- On-screen text and close-up or medium framing (Ling et al.) carry over to animation: keep characters large in frame and use text overlays.

### Gaps
- No academic study was found on **animated** short-form comedy specifically, or on Brazilian or Portuguese-language short video.
- Direction and effect sizes for Ling et al.'s features, and Bai's per-feature results, were not readable in this session.
- No peer-reviewed study on duets or stitches, or on trending sounds as predictors, was found in this session.

## 3. Retention mechanics (hooks, open loops, payoff, loops, length, pacing)

### Takeaway
Nearly all hook, length and loop numbers come from vendors or platform relays, not peer review. They agree on direction:
- Most drop-off happens in the first 2-5 seconds.
- Shorter videos complete more often.
- 20-45 s is commonly cited as the sweet spot for clipped Shorts.
- A seamless ending-to-start loop boosts replays.

The figures differ wildly between sources, so treat them as directional. A 30-40 s episode sits at the long end of "short". It needs a hook in the first 1-2 s and sustained escalation.

### Cited Findings
- **[VENDOR] OpusClip**:
  - 50-60% of the viewers who drop off do so in the first 3 seconds (analysis of "thousands" of Shorts).
  - It recommends completion above 60% for Shorts under 30 s, and says 15-30 s Shorts "often exceed 80% retention".
  - It describes a "loop effect" (the ending flows back into the start) and says "even a 10% replay rate can significantly boost" distribution.

  Secondary sites attribute to OpusClip a "76% watch-through and 1.7M average views for 30-45 s Shorts", but I could not find this on OpusClip's page. Unverified. — [OpusClip blog](https://www.opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention) (search summary)
- **[VENDOR]** vidIQ:
  - The highest-retention zone for clipped Shorts is 20-45 s.
  - The best length is "the shortest one that delivers the idea clearly".
  - Since 31 March 2025, YouTube counts every Short start or replay as a view, with no minimum watch time.
  - "Viewed vs. swiped away" is the key early metric.

  — [vidIQ, Shorts algorithm](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/) (search summary); [vidIQ, clipping](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/Clip-youtube-videos-long-to-short/) (search summary)
- **[PLATFORM, relayed by an agency]** A Meta figure: 65% of people who watch the first 3 s of a video watch at least 10 s, and 45% watch 30 s. Relayed, not opened from Meta. — [Percee Digital](https://www.perceedigital.com/insights/video-hooks-that-convert/) (search summary)
- **[VENDOR, relayed]** Zebracat 2025: Shorts with an immediate hook in the first 2 s retain 19% more viewers. — [Yiha.ai blog](https://yiha.ai/blogs/hook-timing-in-short-form-video-first-3-seconds-retention-rules) (search summary)
- **[VENDOR]** MuseOn analysis of 10,000 TikTok videos: **5-second retention correlates r = 0.83 with total watch depth**. The 5 s mark may matter as much as 3 s, and short video is "hooked or skipped" with little middle ground. — [MuseOn](https://www.museon.ai/blog/hooked-or-skipped) (search summary)
- **[VENDOR]** Benchmark of 50,000+ assets:
  - average 3 s hold of 50-58%;
  - average completion of 18-24%;
  - top-20% performers complete at 28-38%.

  This contradicts the "60-80% completion" targets above. The metric definitions probably differ. — [Grow-it benchmarks](https://www.grow-it.se/en/resources/short-form-video-retention-benchmarks) (search summary)
- **[VENDOR, relayed]** Socialinsider 2024 (cited by an agency): TikTok videos under 15 s complete at 92%, and 31-60 s videos fall to 68%. Another 2026 Shorts dataset puts under-20 s at about 68% and over-60 s at about 41%. These two sets conflict on absolute levels but agree on the direction (shorter means higher completion). — [Grow-it](https://www.grow-it.se/en/resources/short-form-video-retention-benchmarks) (search summary); [Virvid](https://virvid.ai/blog/best-shorts-length-retention-2026) (search summary)
- **[LORE]** Looping structure: the last line or image sets up the first, so a replay feels seamless and continuous. It is described as a "hidden retention trick". — [Virvid, looping structure](https://virvid.ai/blog/looping-structure-shorts-retention-2026) (search summary)
- **[PEER-review of educational video, not social]** Viewers drop out of learning videos within the first 5 s. This is a different genre. — [arXiv 2301.13617](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.13617) (search summary)
- **[PEER][PRE-SF, ads]** Tellis et al. 2019 found that sharing peaked at moderate length (1.2-1.7 min) for *YouTube ads*. That is not transferable to vertical feeds, but it shows that "shorter is always better" is not a law for sharing, as opposed to completion. — [AMA](https://ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-what-drives-sharing-of-digital-content) (search summary)

### Inferences
- At 30-40 s, the series trades some completion for room to develop a story. The vendor data says this works if the first 2 s show conflict or an absurd image, there is a mid-point escalation, and the payoff lands late. The ending should cut back into the opening line so the episode loops.
- A practical internal metric is to watch 3 s and 5 s retention per episode and treat the 5 s figure as the main health signal (MuseOn r = 0.83).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that compares hook *types* (conflict vs curiosity gap vs face vs text) on short video.
- No reliable data was found on cuts per second or pacing for short-form comedy.
- TikTok Marketing Science reports on hooks (often quoted as "most high-performing ads deliver key message or product in first 3 s") were not located with a URL in this session. Do not cite them without checking.

## 4. Comment and share drivers (relatability, tagging, debate, easter eggs, recurring jokes, part 2)

### Takeaway
The strongest data point says relatability raises comments and lowers shares, while moralized or outgroup content raises both. That raises both comments and shares, but carries reputation risk and is off-brand for light comedy. Evidence on easter eggs, running gags and "part 2" serialization is essentially absent from academic and vendor sources in this session.

### Cited Findings
- **[PREPRINT]** Relatability on TikTok: +3.7% to +8.0% comments per view, but −7.4% to −11.3% shares. Outgroup animosity: +20.6% comments and +5.0% shares. — [arXiv 2509.10336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.10336) (search summary)
- **[PREPRINT]** Dark humor on YouTube Shorts produces more mixed and sometimes toxic comment threads than regular humor. Debate-style comments come at a moderation cost. — [arXiv 2606.00046](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00046) (opened)
- **[PEER]** Warren & McGraw 2016: severe humorous violations can hurt the brand even when they are funny. This is the controversy risk for a brand-adjacent series. — [Now Publishers](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/JMB-0027) (search summary)
- **[PEER][PRE-SF]** Tellis et al.: characters and plot (drama) increase sharing of video content. This is the closest evidence that recurring characters help. — [AMA](https://ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-what-drives-sharing-of-digital-content) (search summary)
- **[PREPRINT]** Micro-drama production on social media, "Audience in the Loop": creators use viewer feedback (comments) to drive serialized content creation. Only the title was seen; details not read. — [arXiv 2602.14045](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.14045) (search summary)

### Inferences
- Use relatable shop-owner pain for the comments ("todo dono de loja já ouviu isso"). Pair it with one sharable surprise or escalation, because relatability alone appears to suppress shares.
- Avoid outgroup or moral-outrage bait. It works in politics, but the brand-harm research and the toxic-comment findings argue against it for this series.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on "tag someone who..." prompts, easter eggs that reward rewatching, running gags that audiences anticipate, or "part 2" cliffhangers. Evidence here would have to come from creator interviews, which were not reached in this session.

## 5. Humor-specific evidence: which comedy formats travel on short-form

### Takeaway
Humor is one of the most reliable engagement predictors on TikTok across domains. Affiliative, everyday-situation humor dominates. Imitating native templates (POV, recognizable formats) helps. No source in this session tested specific formats such as POV, "type of person", "when the customer says..." or recurring-character sketches against each other.

### Cited Findings
- **[PEER]** Humor and imitation predict views, likes, comments and shares (1,025 TikTok videos). — [PubMed 42533308](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42533308/) (search summary)
- **[PEER]** Comedy leads popularity in TikTok news content (N = 1,000). — [SciTePress](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2025/138346/138346.pdf) (search summary)
- **[PEER]** Spanish TikTok humor is mainly affiliative humor about everyday situations. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379758820_El_humor_en_TikTok_un_caso_de_estudio_de_cinco_creadores_de_contenido_espanoles) (search summary)
- **[PREPRINT]** Point of view is an influential virality feature (Ling et al., N = 400; direction not stated). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02452) (opened)
- **[PEER] Library-account study**: humanizing and fun videos received the highest engagement (cited in a search summary, institutional accounts). — URL not isolated; seen in the summary of the search that returned [Social Science Computer Review 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/08944393231178603) (search summary; attribution uncertain)
- **[PEER][PRE-SF]** Benign violation: humor needs a violation plus benignness, and psychological distance helps. — [McGraw & Warren 2010](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf) (search summary)

### Inferences
- Workplace and customer humor ("when the customer asks for a discount on a R$2 item") is affiliative, everyday and mildly transgressive. It fits both the TikTok evidence and benign violation theory.
- POV framing and recognizable format templates are low-cost ways to gain the "imitation" advantage.

### Gaps
- No comparative data was found on POV vs sketch vs "type of person" formats, or on recurring characters in short-form, except Tellis's general "characters raise sharing" result for ads.

## 6. Saves and practical value

### Takeaway
The only rigorous evidence (Tellis et al.) found that informational content *reduces* sharing of video, except for risky or new products. Practical value inside entertainment therefore has to be incidental and must not interrupt the joke. No data was found on whether comedy gets saved.

### Cited Findings
- **[PEER][PRE-SF, online video ads]** Information-focused content has a significant negative effect on sharing, except in risky contexts or when the product is new or unfamiliar. — [AMA](https://ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-what-drives-sharing-of-digital-content) (search summary)
- **[PEER]** Humor does not guarantee the target behavior: funny PSAs may not change behavior (McGraw). — [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/asmagazine-archive/node/1303) (search summary)

### Inferences
- If an episode embeds something useful, for example "prefeitura publica edital de compras pequenas", it should be the joke's premise or punchline, never a tagged-on explanation. Tellis's "new or unfamiliar product" exception hints that the *existence* of public tenders for small shops, if genuinely news to the audience, might be the one kind of information that does not depress sharing. This is speculative.

### Gaps
- No platform or vendor data on save rates for comedy vs educational content was found in this session.
- Berger's "practical value" component of STEPPS was not sourced here.
