# Joke and Sketch Structure: Craft Mechanics for a 30-Second, 8-11 Line Dialogue Sketch

Reading notes on source quality, which apply to every section:

- **[opened]** means I fetched and read the page. **[snippet]** means the claim comes from a search-result excerpt and the page itself was not opened (or was blocked), so the exact wording and the page it came from are less certain.
- Almost none of the books were available as primary text. Most book-level claims come from secondary summaries, reviews, or the author's own blog. The exception is Johnstone's *Impro*, quoted from a full-text scan.
- Sections marked "Inferences" are my own application to the 30-second format and are not sourced claims.

## 1. How do setup/punchline, misdirection, the rule of three, escalation, callbacks and tags work mechanically? What makes a punchline land?

### Takeaway
A joke builds one expectation and then forces a re-reading of it; the laugh comes at the moment of re-reading, so everything about wording serves to delay and sharpen that moment (shortest path to the turn, surprise word last, no telegraphing). Greg Dean's model gives the most precise vocabulary for the mechanism, and Toplyn's and Trapp's rules give the most usable editing checklist.

### Cited Findings

**Greg Dean's joke anatomy (from *Step by Step to Stand-Up Comedy*, as summarized by Scotty Meltzer)**
- Setup: the first part of the joke, which makes the audience think it is heading one way when it is going another. Punch: the second part, which gets the laugh and triggers the re-reading. [opened] — [Meltzer, "Nice Structure", juggle.org](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure/)
- The setup creates a "1st Story" in the audience's head built on an assumption (Dean: the "Target Assumption" in his terminology, per the query framing; Meltzer's write-up labels the broken assumption the "Decoy Assumption" and the revealed one the "Target Assumption" and uses his own word "Shatter"). The terminology mapping differs between Dean's book and Meltzer's paraphrase, so check the book before quoting labels. [opened] — [Meltzer, juggle.org](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure/)
- Connector: Dean's coined term for "the thing in your setup that is perceived in two ways." It can be a word, phrase, gesture, object, or idea. The punch makes the audience reinterpret the connector ("The Reinterpretation"), producing the "2nd Story". [opened] — [Meltzer, juggle.org](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure/)
- Worked example (Meltzer's, not Dean's): Setup "Got a lighter?" Connector: the reason for asking. Reinterpretation: the speaker is stealing it. Punch: "Got a watch?" [opened] — [Meltzer, juggle.org](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure/)
- Dean's own canonical example reportedly uses "dinner" as the connector with the punch "He was delicious." [snippet] — [Meltzer, "You Punch Like a Girl", juggle.org](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)
- Give the minimum information needed for the audience to jump from assumption to shatter; target a specific person or show rather than a group or genre. [snippet] — [Meltzer, juggle.org](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)
- Linguistics describes the same mechanism as "frame-shifting": an ambiguous element forces the listener to reinterpret what came before. [snippet] — [Coulson, Urbach & Kutas](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/Papers/coulson-urbach-kutas.pdf)

**Joe Toplyn (*Comedy Writing for Late-Night TV*; ex-Letterman/Leno head writer)**
- A joke = Topic (the setup), Angle (the link from topic to punch line), Punch Line. Punch lines are generated from "handles" (the key words of the topic) and their well-known "associations" (snail: slimy, slow, eaten by the French). [opened] — [Toplyn, "How to Write a Joke"](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke/)
- Keep the topic simple, with "only one or two obvious topic handles." [opened] — [Toplyn](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke/)
- "I backload the Topic, moving the most important elements closer to the Punch Line." [opened] — [Toplyn](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke/)
- Joke Maximizers named on his blog: "Make everything clear" (#4) and "Wildly exaggerate" (#8). [opened] — [Toplyn](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke/)
- Further Joke Maximizers, from blog excerpts: shorten as much as possible (#1); end on the laugh trigger, defined as the most unexpected word or two of the punch line (#2); don't telegraph the punch line, because a predicted punch line gets no laugh (#5); make the punch line parallel, e.g. "Yard High Club" matching the syllables of "Mile High Club" (#6); use stop consonants (B, D, G, K, P, T), alliteration and assonance (#7); use the rule of three with the laugh trigger as the third item (#10); don't be too on-the-nose (#11). [snippet] — [Toplyn blog, joke-writing workshop](https://joetoplyn.com/category/joke-writing-workshop)
- The book has six "Punch Line Makers"; the ones identifiable from the blog are: link associations of two handles (#1), use a pop-culture association (#2), ask a question about a handle and answer with an association (#3), wordplay on a double meaning (#4). [opened/snippet] — [Toplyn](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke/)

**Mike Trapp (CollegeHumor/Dropout writer; "Chuffah" newsletter) on line-level craft**
- End on the best word: put the surprise at the end of the line. "Let your punchlines punch." [opened] — [Trapp, "How to make your lines as funny as..."](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Leave the joke unexplained: "Give them only half the equation." [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Keep it tight: "Subvert expectations as quickly as you set them." [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Fewer words, shorter words, simpler sentences: "That guy ain't so smart" beats "That gentleman isn't very intelligent"; "Simple sentences are funnier sentences." [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Specificity as word choice: test synonyms for the funniest one (goose vs. swan, slurp vs. drink, bees vs. hornets). [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Parallelism: repeat structure to set a pattern; "Make your wording and grammar consistent." [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Rhyme, alliteration and references are "sprinkles," not the joke; "Don't settle for almost-a-joke." Memes, stereotypes and hack jokes are "zombie jokes" with no surprise. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)

**Rule of three (the "triple")**
- A triple is three examples in sequence; "the triple increases tension with its longer buildup." The first two establish the pattern, the last is the most audacious, and the funniest item goes last. [opened] — [Shatz, Writer's Digest, adapted from Shatz & Helitzer, *Comedy Writing Secrets*](https://writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)
- Why three and not more: "there's no reason to give five examples when three accomplishes all the preparation that's needed." Two items is "softer" and lacks tension. [opened] — [Shatz, Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)
- SAP test (attributed to William Lang): Setup, Anticipation (the triple), Punch line. [opened] — [Shatz, Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)
- Example (Wendy Liebman): "It's a three piece: It's a top, a bottom, and a blindfold for you." [opened] — [Shatz, Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)
- Common combination: triple, then a reverse. [opened] — [Shatz, Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)
- Contested: Trapp treats the rule as flexible. Because audiences know it, subverting at the second beat or adding a fourth can restore surprise. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)

**Punch-word placement and K-sounds**
- The funniest word belongs at the end of the sentence; a reveal word in mid-sentence gives the joke away. [snippet] — [Writer's Digest, "Word Play"](https://writersdigest.com/humor/word-play)
- The same advice from Toastmasters: place the punch word as close to the end as possible. [snippet] — [Toastmasters Magazine, July 2018](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2018/july2018/humor)
- K-sounds, contested. For: Writer's Digest treats the "k" sound as a real aid, and Toplyn generalizes it to all stop consonants (B, D, G, K, P, T). [snippet] — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/humor/word-play); [Toplyn blog](https://joetoplyn.com/category/joke-writing-workshop). Against: Trapp calls the idea that K sounds are inherently funny "absolute horse shit," while still advising writers to test synonyms. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)

**Callbacks**
- For endings, look for something that happens or is mentioned early that can be brought back at the end. [snippet] — [search excerpt from sketch-writing guides; likely GOLD Comedy](https://goldcomedy.com/resources/how-to-write-a-comedy-sketch)

### Inferences
- In an 8-11 line sketch there is room for roughly one full Dean-style misdirection (one connector) plus the game's escalation. Use the misdirection either as the first unusual thing (line 2-3) or as the button, not both.
- A triple fits naturally across three speaking turns: lines N, N+1 normal, N+2 breaks the pattern. In Portuguese the "reveal word last" rule needs care, since natural word order often puts the object or adjective last anyway; rewrite until the surprise noun is the final word of the line and nothing follows it (no "né", no trailing clause).
- "Backload the topic" translates to dialogue as: the feed line should end on the word the punch line plays against.
- Treat hard consonants as a tie-breaker between two equally good words only; the evidence for it is practitioner folklore.

### Gaps
- I did not find a sourced, mechanical definition of "tags/toppers" (extra punch lines added after a punch line without a new setup). The term is standard stand-up vocabulary, but no opened source defined it, so it is unsourced here.
- The full list of Toplyn's six Punch Line Makers and twelve-ish Joke Maximizers was not retrievable outside the book; numbering above is partial.
- Dean's exact definitions were not read in the book itself; Meltzer's paraphrase relabels some terms.
- Gene Perret: no source found in this research. Nothing here should be attributed to him.

## 2. What is the "game of the scene" (UCB)? How do sketch writers find and play one game?

### Takeaway
UCB's method: build a believable ordinary world, let exactly one unusual thing break it, then repeat and escalate that one pattern instead of adding new ideas. For a 30-second sketch this means one game, started within the first few lines, with three or so heightened beats.

### Cited Findings
- Definition from the manual, quoted in reader's notes: "Game is what is funny about your scene." Game is "a consistent pattern of behavior that breaks from the expected patterns of our everyday lives." [opened, secondary notes on the book] — [Notes on the UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual](https://notes.bijouede.com/3+Resources/comedy/Book+-+UCB+Improv+Manual)
- The stated benefit: focusing on one comic idea means you don't have to keep hunting for new funny material. The manual's chapter order is Base Reality, creating Base Reality, Game, Finding Game, Playing Game. [opened] — [Notes on the UCB manual](https://notes.bijouede.com/3+Resources/comedy/Book+-+UCB+Improv+Manual)
- Base reality: a believable situation that isn't trying to be funny, an ordinary world against which the unusual stands out; it answers who, what, where. [snippet] — [UCB class handout (Google Doc)](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1fH6KU9pQbGdHXuAS03ZjcsLEcNVO2n70paZpXUbJj4k/export?format=pdf)
- First unusual thing: players "Yes And" until the first break from the ordinary appears. If the base reality is itself funny, the unusual is baked in and nothing can register as unusual. [snippet] — [UCB class handout (Google Doc)](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1fH6KU9pQbGdHXuAS03ZjcsLEcNVO2n70paZpXUbJj4k/export?format=pdf)
- The two "If, Then" questions: "If this is true, then what else is true?" (heightening) and "If this is true, then why is it true?" (exploring/justification). Example: an air hangar full of dragons leads to a Pegasus for stealth operations. [snippet; the heightening/exploring labelling comes from a secondary source] — [Authentic Comedy, "If this is true what else is true?"](https://authenticcomedy.substack.com/p/lessons-stand-ups-can-learn-from-ff6)
- "Play at the top of your intelligence": characters react as real people would. A UK improv teacher's UCB 101 notes: "Have basic agreement with the reality presented... just respond as if it's real." [opened] — [Hoopla Impro, "Things I learnt from the UCB 101 week"](https://www.hooplaimpro.com/?p=1057)
- Dissent within improv: the same teacher argues "The Game of the Scene is fun, but more important is the reality of the scene and the relationships in the first place." He also links "if this is true, what else is true" to Keith Johnstone's "circle of expectation." [opened] — [Hoopla Impro](https://www.hooplaimpro.com/?p=1057)

**Applying game to written sketches (Mike Trapp)**
- Finding the game: work out why the first beat is funny. Identify the "surprising truth," then the unusual behavior that shows it, then the aspect of that behavior to repeat and heighten. [opened] — [Trapp, "8 common sketch problems"](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- If every beat repeats the first, the game is stated too narrowly: restate it as a broader pattern and vary the specifics while keeping the same surprising truth. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- Start the game within 3-5 lines (this is for a five-page sketch). More than five beats is too many; the gaps between beats should shrink as the sketch goes on, and "five lines between your first two beats is probably too much." [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- Commit to one game; cut anything that doesn't support it. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- Heighten boldly and fast, staying slightly ahead of the audience; slowing down in revision is easier than speeding up. Break predictable beats with misdirects, side jokes and other characters' reactions. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- If the audience is confused, the first beat was heightened too quickly: make the first beat relatable or justify the behavior with a real-world connection. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- Straight man: gives the audience a reality anchor and underlines the unusual behavior. Give them a specific reason to oppose the game; have them act intelligently and proactively rather than just say "this is crazy!" A sketch can drop the straight man if something else establishes reality. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- Don't add a second unusual thing. [snippet] — [search excerpt from sketch-writing guides; likely Jimmy Carrane, "5 keys to writing a great sketch"](https://jimmycarrane.com/5-keys-to-writing-a-great-sketch/)

### Inferences
- Scaled to 8-11 lines: base reality in line 1 (one line, who/what/where carried by the animation and the recurring cast), first unusual thing by line 2 or 3, then three heightened beats of about two lines each (move plus reaction), then the button. That leaves no room for a second game or for "exploring" at length; one short justification line ("why is it true?") is the most the format affords and often supplies the best laugh because it reveals the character's logic.
- With 2-4 recurring characters, the base reality is prepaid: the audience already knows who is who, so the game can start at line 1 or 2 after the first few episodes.
- State the game in one sentence before writing ("X treats Y as if it were Z"). If the sentence needs "and", there are two games.
- Recurring characters invite a recurring game per character (the same pattern in a new situation each episode), which is how UCB-style "what else is true" extends across episodes.

### Gaps
- The UCB manual's own text was not available; framing, "resting the game," and the manual's exact wording on straight man/voice of reason are unverified here. The Google Doc handout could not be opened (export blocked), so its content rests on a search excerpt and its authorship is unknown.
- No Second City or Groundlings writing guide was reached.

## 3. What does Keith Johnstone teach about status transactions and how do status shifts create comedy?

### Takeaway
For Johnstone, status is something characters do to each other line by line, not a rank they hold, and comedy is largely the business of lowering someone's status. The funniest configuration is a small status gap that keeps tipping, or a mismatch between social rank and played status.

### Cited Findings
All quotes below are from the text of *Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre* (Faber and Faber, 1979), Status chapter, read from a full-text scan. [opened]
- "Status is a confusing term unless it's understood as something one does." — [Johnstone, *Impro* (archive.org scan)](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- The see-saw principle: "I go up and you go down." — [Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- "A comedian is someone paid to lower his own or other people's status." — [Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- Keep the gap small: actors "have to get their status just a little above or below their partner's." — [Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- "When a very high-status person is wiped out, everyone feels pleasure as they experience the feeling of moving up a step." — [Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- "Audiences enjoy a contrast between the status played and the social status" (his example is a tramp who plays high status to a social superior). — [Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- "Every inflection of the voice implies a status." — [Johnstone, *Impro*](https://archive.org/stream/pdfy-KIkLrSPi_3lzPl5v/Keith%20Johnstone-Impro_%20Improvisation%20and%20the%20Theatre-Methuen%20Drama%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)
- Status moves hide inside ordinary small talk. Johnstone's example: Mrs X says "I had a nasty turn last week... I thought I should faint or something" and he glosses it "Mrs X is attempting to raise her status"; Mrs Y tops her with "You're lucky to have been going to a cinema." [opened, quoted secondhand] — [LessWrong, "The Many Faces of Status"](https://www.lesswrong.com/lw/23h/the_many_faces_of_status/1vyy)
- Objection: the see-saw is not strictly zero-sum; it fails when you identify with the person being raised or lowered. [snippet] — [LessWrong discussion](https://www.lesswrong.com/lw/23h/the_many_faces_of_status/1vz4)
- Status connects to sketch ethics: mocking high-status targets inverts expectations, mocking low-status ones reads as bullying. This is Trapp's point, not Johnstone's. [opened] — [Trapp, "8 common sketch problems"](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)

### Inferences
- A status flip is a ready-made button: the character who held high status for nine lines is dropped in the last one (or the low-status one calmly takes over). Johnstone's "moving up a step" pleasure explains why this ending satisfies.
- For recurring characters, fix each one's default status and preferred status move (who condescends, who apologizes, who refuses to notice). The episode's comedy can then come from the moment the default breaks.
- Since "every inflection implies a status," status can be rewritten at the word level: who interrupts, who asks permission, who names the other, who gets the last word. In animation, that also means who moves and who stays still.
- Status and game combine: the absurd character usually plays unearned high status about their absurd thing, and the straight character loses status each time they fail to stop it.

### Gaps
- Only the first fifth of the scanned text was read, so the master-servant material and the list of concrete high/low status behaviors (eye contact, head stillness, hesitation sounds) are not quoted here.

## 4. What do the comedy-writing teachers say about comic premise and character?

### Takeaway
The teachers converge on one idea in different vocabularies: a comic character is an ordinary person with a strong skewed way of seeing, who wants something, lacks what it takes to get it, and keeps trying anyway. The second character's job is to see the problem and fail to fix it.

### Cited Findings

**Steve Kaplan, *The Hidden Tools of Comedy* (2013)**
- The comedy equation: an ordinary guy or gal struggling "against insurmountable odds, without many of the required tools with which to win, yet never giving up hope." [opened, review] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Winning: "Comedy gives the character permission to win", meaning the character may do anything, however foolish, that they believe will get them what they want. [opened] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Non-Hero: not a fool, "simply somebody who lacks something." The more skills the character has, the less comic the scene. [opened] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- "A Non-Hero doesn't need to try to be funny, just to not know." [snippet] — [Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/21842865)
- Positive action: "every action your character takes, your character actually thinks is going to work" (Basil Fawlty, Louie DePalma). [opened] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Active emotion: "the emotion that occurs naturally in the course of trying to win", as opposed to performed funny feeling. [opened] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Metaphorical relationship: "beneath every surface relationship is a true, or essential, metaphorical relationship" (Oscar and Felix as an old married couple; Jerry and George as kids in the back of a police car). [opened] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Straight line/wavy line: comedy is "watcher and watched." The Straight Line creates the problem or is blind to it; the Wavy Line sees it and struggles without solving it. Kaplan argues the traditional "straight man" label misleads: in "Who's on First?" the person usually called the straight man is the one causing the problem. [opened] — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- Kaplan credits the watcher idea to a John Cleese remark about early Monty Python. [snippet] — [Kaplan blog via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/24701769-kaplan-writes-funny)
- Kaplan's remaining two tools (archetypes, comic premise) were not described in the sources read.

**John Vorhaus, *The Comic Toolbox* (1994)**
- Four-part comic character: comic perspective, exaggeration, flaws, humanity. Comic perspective is the character's unique lens on the world; the further from ordinary, the stronger. Exaggeration pushes that perspective as far as it goes (Costanza's selfishness, Scrooge's greed). [opened, secondary blog which misnames the book "The Comic Toolkit" and covers only the first two parts] — [The Duck Webcomics](https://next.theduckwebcomics.com/news/2017/apr/20/comedy-part-two-creating-comedic-characters)
- Comic premise as the gap between a comic reality and real reality, with exaggeration and "clash of context" as tools. [snippet] — [Jacqui Murray blog](https://jacquimurray.net/2025/04/25/how-to-be-funny-if-youre-not-ii/)

**Scott Sedita, *The Eight Characters of Comedy***
- The eight: Lovable Loser (pursues schemes that fail), Logical Smart One (grounded, sarcastic, cleans up), Neurotic (controlling, driven by fear), Bitch/Bastard (mean in ways that get laughs), Dumb One, In Their Own Universe (a skewed worldview that makes sense to them), Womanizer/Manizer, Materialistic One. [opened, a screenwriter's summary] — [Flying Wrestler (Erik Bork)](https://www.flyingwrestler.com/2015/12/8-characters-of-comedy/)
- Usage notes from the same summary: give each character a clear baseline type; a type can shift with scene partner; characters "need to be mostly failing"; most good characters blend types. [opened] — [Flying Wrestler](https://www.flyingwrestler.com/2015/12/8-characters-of-comedy/)

**Scott Dikkers (The Onion founder), *How to Write Funny***
- Method: start from subtext, "the hidden message or opinion underlying a joke," then run it through one or more of 11 Funny Filters. [opened, summary excerpt] — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/how-to-write-funny-summary-scott-dikkers)
- The eleven filters by name: Irony, Character, Shock, Hyperbole, Wordplay, Reference, Madcap, Parody, Analogy, Misplaced Focus, Metahumor. [opened] — [Dikkers Substack](https://scottdikkers.substack.com/p/remembering-all-eleven)

**Judy Carter, *The Comedy Bible***
- Start from topics that genuinely bother, confuse or hurt you, and attach a specific emotion (attitude) to the bit. [snippet] — [judycarter.com](https://www.judycarter.com/blog/writing-stand-up-comedy-for-beginners)
- Jokes run setup, punchline, act-out (performing the situation as the characters). Cut anything that takes more than three lines to reach the punchline. [snippet, from AI-generated summary sites, low reliability] — [BeFreed summary](https://www.befreed.ai/book/the-comedy-bible-by-judy-carter)

**Jerry Corley**
- Strong jokes usually contain at least two "laughter triggers"; the named ones include surprise, superiority, incongruity and configuration. [snippet] — [Backstage, "How to Write a Joke"](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/art-writing-joke-742/)
- Incongruity as imposing the characteristics of one thing onto another; listen for words that can carry a double meaning. [snippet] — [Toastmasters, "Cracking the Laughter Code"](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2018/aug2018/cracking-the-laughter-code)

### Inferences
- Kaplan's straight/wavy line and UCB's unusual character/voice of reason describe the same pair from opposite ends. The practical merge: the absurd character is calm and certain (Straight Line, positive action, never winks), and the laugh often lands on the other character's helpless reaction (Wavy Line). In a short animated sketch, the reaction line or reaction shot is where to place the beat.
- "Non-hero doesn't know" is the cheapest fix for an unfunny line: remove the character's awareness that they are being funny. No character in the sketch should be joking.
- For a fixed cast of 2-4, Sedita's types work as a casting grid: one Logical Smart One as reality anchor plus one or two from Lovable Loser / Neurotic / In Their Own Universe gives every pairing a built-in Straight/Wavy relation. Avoid two characters of the same type.
- Vorhaus's perspective sentence ("X sees everything as Y") and the UCB game sentence are interchangeable planning tools; a recurring character's perspective is their recurring game.
- Kaplan's metaphorical relationship is a fast premise generator for recurring pairs: coworkers who behave like an old married couple, a boss and intern who behave like parent and toddler.
- Dikkers's filters work as a rewrite menu for a flat line: try the same subtext as hyperbole, as misplaced focus, as irony, and keep the best.

### Gaps
- Vorhaus's "flaws" and "humanity" definitions were not found in any opened source. His book was not read directly.
- Dikkers's definitions of each filter were paywalled; only names are confirmed.
- Gene Perret: nothing found. Corley's list of joke structures and Carter's formulas were found only in excerpts or unreliable summary sites.
- Kaplan's and Sedita's material comes from reviews and summaries, not the books.

## 5. Why are things funny (incongruity, benign violation, superiority, relief) and what does that imply in practice?

### Takeaway
Incongruity is the dominant account but incomplete on its own, since a violated expectation can also produce fear or disgust; benign violation theory adds the missing condition that the violation must simultaneously feel safe. Practically: make something wrong, and make sure it is wrong in a harmless way.

### Cited Findings
- Incongruity theory "is now the dominant theory of humor in philosophy and psychology." Core claim: humor arises from perceiving something that violates our mental patterns and expectations. Kant: "Laughter is an affection arising from the sudden transformation of a strained expectation into nothing." [opened] — [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Philosophy of Humor"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/humor/)
- Objection: incongruity is not sufficient, because violations can equally produce fear, disgust or anger. [opened] — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/humor/)
- Incongruity-resolution: Schultz and Suls argue "what we enjoy in humor is not incongruity itself, but the resolution of incongruity." Others reply that resolution features in only some humor. [opened] — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/humor/)
- Superiority theory (Plato, Hobbes): laughter expresses a feeling of superiority over others or a former self; Hobbes's "sudden glory." Objection (Hutcheson): superiority is neither necessary nor sufficient. [opened] — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/humor/)
- Relief theory (Shaftesbury, Spencer, Freud): laughter discharges built-up nervous energy. The entry says it is "seldom used as a general explanation" because the hydraulic model is outdated. [opened] — [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/humor/)
- Benign violation theory (A. Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren, 2010): humor occurs when a situation is appraised as a violation, as benign, and both at once. A violation can seem benign through an alternative norm that makes it acceptable, weak commitment to the violated norm, or psychological distance. [snippet of the paper; the PDF could not be read] — [McGraw & Warren 2010, *Psychological Science*](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf)
- Distance cuts both ways (McGraw, Warren, Williams & Leonard, 2012): distance makes tragedies funnier by reducing threat, while closeness makes mild mishaps funnier by keeping some threat. A violation that is too harmless or too severe is not funny. [snippet] — [McGraw et al. 2012](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.williams.leonard.2012.pdf)
- Caveat: the empirical support for benign violation comes largely from the theory's own proponents. [snippet-based assessment] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01380/full)

### Inferences
- Incongruity-resolution maps onto Dean's joke: setup creates the expectation, the punch violates it, the connector lets the audience resolve it. A punchline that is only random (incongruity with no resolution path) is the "confusing" failure; one that is fully predictable has no incongruity.
- Benign violation gives a two-question test for each beat: What is wrong here? Why is it okay? If nothing is wrong, the beat is flat; if it is not okay, the beat is uncomfortable. Cartoon abstraction is itself a distancing device, which lets animated characters go further than live actors before a violation stops being benign.
- The 2012 distance finding suggests calibrating by severity: small everyday annoyances should be played close and specific (real brands, real phrases, recognizable workplace detail), and anything dark should be pushed away (hypothetical, exaggerated, happening to a cartoon).
- Superiority explains the non-hero and Johnstone's status drop: the audience enjoys knowing more than the character. It works best aimed at a character's self-importance, and worst aimed at a weak target.
- Relief theory is weak science but useful stagecraft: a beat of tension (a pause, an awkward silence, a threat of conflict) before the punch gives the laugh something to release.

### Gaps
- McGraw's primary papers and the Humor Research Lab site could not be read directly (PDF not extractable, press page blocked). The three "benign" routes are reported from a search excerpt and should be checked against the paper before being quoted.
- No source tested these theories on very short video sketches specifically.

## 6. Most common mistakes that make sketches unfunny; how to write a strong button

### Takeaway
Most failed sketches have a structural problem and not a joke problem: a late start, more than one game, slow or repetitive heightening, a straight man who only objects, or no ending. A button is one last line or action that either tops the game or flips it, and practitioners agree it is the hardest part to get right.

### Cited Findings

**Mistakes and fixes (Mike Trapp's eight, written for five-page sketches)** [opened] — [Trapp, "8 common sketch problems and how to fix them"](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- No idea where to go after the first beat: you have not identified why the first beat is funny.
- Every beat repeats the first: the game is defined too narrowly.
- Too long: too many beats (over five), too much space between beats, a slow start (the game should begin within 3-5 lines), or more than one game.
- Boring: heightening too slowly or too predictably.
- Confusing: weak point of view, or heightened too far too soon without a relatable first beat.
- Straight man slows it down: the character is unnecessary, has no motive for objecting, or behaves stupidly (repeating "this is crazy!").
- Reads as mean: the target is lower status than the sketch assumes.
- Can't find an ending: usually the writer is bored with the sketch. Try a beat so heightened it feels risky, or a swing so unexpected it seems to break the sketch.

**Other commonly cited mistakes** [snippet; from search excerpts of sketch-writing guides, individual pages not opened]
- Overwriting: "If a joke takes four sentences to set up, it's too long." — [Final Draft, "10 tips from the skit masters"](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/10-sketch-writing-tips-on-writing-from-sketch-masters)
- Talking heads: two people on a couch discussing something funny that happened off-screen is a podcast, not a sketch. — [Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/10-sketch-writing-tips-on-writing-from-sketch-masters)
- Explaining the joke: covered under Trapp's "Give them only half the equation." [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Ending on a pun tends to get a groan and not a laugh. — [GOLD Comedy, "How to write a comedy sketch"](https://goldcomedy.com/resources/how-to-write-a-comedy-sketch)

**Buttons and endings**
- A button is a sharp final line or action that sums up the theme or adds one last escalation. A strong ending lifts the whole sketch; a weak one can let down an otherwise strong sketch. [snippet] — [GOLD Comedy](https://goldcomedy.com/resources/how-to-write-a-comedy-sketch)
- Among sketch writers, script editors and producers, "the final punchline is by far the hardest element to get right." The rule of thumb offered is that it should be "surprising yet inevitable." [opened, rest of article paywalled] — [British Comedy Guide, "Sketch writing: final punchline tips"](https://comedy.co.uk/pro/inside_track/sketch-writing-final-punchline-tips)
- Seven ways to end a sketch (Chris Head, comedy script editor): [opened] — [British Comedy Guide, "Seven ways to end a sketch"](https://comedy.co.uk/pro/inside_track/seven-ways-to-end-a-sketch)
  1. Normality restored: snap back to normal after the chaos.
  2. Reversal: the reasonable character adopts the unreasonable one's behavior (Key & Peele's Substitute Teacher: a pupil finally answers to the mispronounced name).
  3. False dawn: the problem appears solved, then resumes (Rowan Atkinson's "Fatal Beatings").
  4. New character: someone enters who either doesn't know what happened or turns out to share the absurdity (Key & Peele: the bully's father talks just like the bully).
  5. Violence: one character hits the other (a Fry & Laurie staple).
  6. Variation: a final variation on the main joke.
  7. Meta: the sketch acknowledges its own artifice.
- Some writers start from the ending and work backwards; this is forum opinion and not an established rule. [snippet] — [British Comedy Guide forum](https://www.comedy.co.uk/forums/post/1136589/)

### Inferences
- Trapp's numbers scale down roughly as follows for 30 seconds: game on by line 2-3 instead of line 3-5; three beats instead of five; one line of reaction between beats at most, shrinking to none.
- Head's seven endings rank differently in an 8-11 line recurring format. Most usable in one line: Reversal (straight character caves), Variation/topper (the biggest instance of the game), False dawn (resolved, then one more), and the status flip from section 3. "New character" costs a line of introduction unless it is one of the known recurring cast, in which case it is cheap and strong. "Meta" wears out fast in a series.
- A callback to line 1 or 2 is the cheapest "surprising yet inevitable" button, because the setup is already paid for. In a recurring series, a callback to a previous episode works the same way for returning viewers but must still make sense to new ones.
- "Late start" in this format means any line before the first unusual thing that the picture could have conveyed. Greetings, scene-setting and names are the first cuts.
- After the button, stop: no reaction line, no explanation. If the platform loops, a button that leads back into line 1 is a bonus.

### Gaps
- Chris Head's and BCG's detailed punchline tips are paywalled. The "explain the joke" and "talking heads" mistakes are attributed from search excerpts whose exact source page I could not confirm.
- No source gave data on which ending types perform best; all of this is practitioner opinion.

## 7. How is comedic dialogue edited for rhythm and economy?

### Takeaway
The editing pass is subtractive and positional: cut every word that isn't setup or punch, use short common words, shorten the distance between expectation and reversal, and move the surprise to the last word of the line. Sound-level tricks (hard consonants, matched syllables) are a minor, contested polish step.

### Cited Findings
- Fewer words, shorter words, simpler sentences; cut parentheticals, extra clauses and redundant adjectives. [opened] — [Trapp, "How to make your lines as funny as..."](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Shorten the gap between setup and punchline; a visual and a spoken subversion landing at the same moment is the tightest version. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Use a hard character limit (he suggests Twitter's) as a brevity drill. [opened] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Toplyn's own rewrite process: add a clarifying word where the audience might be lost, remove distracting specifics, repeat the key word the punch line depends on, backload the topic, then shorten. Clarity outranks brevity ("Make everything clear"). [opened] — [Toplyn, "How to Write a Joke"](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke/)
- End on the laugh trigger; don't telegraph; match the rhythm of parallel phrases by syllable count. [snippet] — [Toplyn blog](https://joetoplyn.com/category/joke-writing-workshop)
- A joke is a small story told in as few words as possible. [snippet] — [Writer's Digest, "A Quick Lesson in Word Economy"](https://www.writersdigest.com/improve-my-writing/a-quick-lesson-in-word-economy)
- The triple's rhythm: three is the count that builds tension without impatience; more items overstretch. Anecdotes should use the fewest words necessary, but over-cutting can weaken the effect. [opened] — [Shatz, Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)
- In sketches, the space between beats should shrink as the sketch proceeds. [opened] — [Trapp, "8 common sketch problems"](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/8-common-sketch-problems-and-how)
- K-sounds and stop consonants: see section 1; practitioners disagree. [opened/snippet] — [Trapp](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as); [Toplyn blog](https://joetoplyn.com/category/joke-writing-workshop)

### Inferences
Editing checklist for an 8-11 line, 30-second script (my synthesis of the above):
1. Budget: at a natural speaking pace 30 seconds holds roughly 60-75 spoken words once pauses and reactions are allowed for, so lines average 6-8 words. This is my estimate, not a sourced figure; time the read aloud.
2. For each line, mark it S (setup), P (punch) or R (reaction). Any line that is none of these is cut.
3. In every P line, underline the surprise word. If it is not the last word, rewrite. Delete everything after it.
4. Check that no feed line contains the punch line's key word or telegraphs the turn.
5. Replace long or abstract words with short concrete ones; replace generic nouns with one specific one.
6. Read aloud for a three-beat pattern and matched syllables in parallel lines.
7. Where a line explains why something is funny, delete the line and let the other character's reaction do it.
8. Give the punch a beat of silence before it and a held reaction after it. In animation this is a timing decision (hold frames) and costs no words, so it should be written into the script as a direction.
9. Confirm lines get shorter toward the end and the button is the shortest strong line in the script.
10. Only then consider sound: between two equal options, prefer the word with harder consonants.

### Gaps
- No opened source gave concrete guidance on pause lengths or words-per-second for comic timing; the word budget above is an estimate.
- Nothing found on rhythm specific to Portuguese-language dialogue, where sentence-final stress and word order differ from the English examples all these sources use.
- Advice specific to animated dialogue timing (as opposed to stage or stand-up) was not found.
